AQA GCSE Physics / Combined Science · Electricity 4.2.2 · Required practical (combinations) · about 25 minutes

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit each component is on its own branch connected across the supply. Switch the branches on and off and watch the meters.

In parallel:
• the p.d. across each branch is the same as the supply p.d.
• the total current is the sum of the branch currents: Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + …
• the total resistance is less than the smallest resistance on its own

Why adding a branch lowers the resistance

Each new branch gives the charge another path to flow through. The supply p.d. across the new branch is the same, so it adds its own current. More current for the same p.d. means a smaller total resistance (R = V ÷ I).

Think of a supermarket

Opening another checkout doesn’t slow the other checkouts down — more people get through every minute. Opening another branch does the same for charge.

Why houses are wired in parallel

Every appliance gets the full mains p.d., and each one can be switched on and off separately. If one bulb breaks, the others stay on.

At GCSE you don’t need an equation for resistors in parallel. You do need to explain why the total is less than either resistor, and you can find it from R = V ÷ Itotal.

Series vs parallel — the practical

The resistance required practical also asks you to compare two resistors in series and in parallel. Measure V and I for the whole combination, then R = V ÷ I.

Exam practice

Lesson summary

  • Parallel: each branch has the full supply p.d.
  • Total current = sum of branch currents. Use I = V ÷ R for each branch.
  • Adding a branch adds another path → total current up → total resistance down.
  • Total resistance in parallel is less than the smallest individual resistance.
