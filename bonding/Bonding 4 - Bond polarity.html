Section 1

Electronegativity

Definition. Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

It is measured on the Pauling scale. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0). Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group.

An atom attracts the bonding pair more strongly when it has more protons in the nucleus and when the bonding pair is closer to the nucleus, with less shielding. That's why small atoms on the right of the periodic table, such as F, O and N, are the most electronegative.

Pauling electronegativity values · darker = more electronegative

You don't need to learn these numbers. You do need to know the trend and which elements are highly electronegative.

Section 2

Polar bonds: where is the electron density?

If the two atoms in a bond have the same electronegativity, the shared pair is shared evenly. If one atom is more electronegative, it pulls the electron density towards itself. The electron distribution is now uneven and the bond is polar.

A bond is only polar if the difference in electronegativity is large. Start each bond on "Shared evenly", then press "Show the reality".

Rule of thumb. A covalent bond is only noticeably polar when one of the atoms is N, O, F, Cl, Br or I and the other is a less electronegative atom such as C or H.
C–H bonds are non-polar. Carbon (2.55) and hydrogen (2.20) are both fairly electronegative, but the difference is too small to make the bond polar. A large difference is what counts, not a large value.

In these run-throughs a difference below about 0.4 is treated as non-polar. A very large difference (as in Na–Cl) means the electron is transferred completely and the bonding is ionic.

Section 3

Showing polarity with partial charges

Polar bonds are shown with partial charges: δ− on the more electronegative atom and δ+ on the other. The δ ("delta") means a small charge, much less than the full charge on an ion.

Don't write H+–Cl−. Full charges mean ions. In a polar covalent bond the electrons are still shared, just unevenly.
Section 4

Is the whole molecule polar?

A molecule has a permanent dipole (it is a polar molecule) only if both of these are true:

Condition 1

It contains polar bonds.

Condition 2

The molecule is unsymmetrical, so the bond dipoles point in an overall direction.

Exam wording. If a molecule has polar bonds but is symmetrical, the bond dipoles cancel out, so the molecule has no permanent dipole.
Molecule checker

Arrows point from δ+ to δ−. The thick arrow is the overall dipole, the sum of all the bond dipoles. In 3D view, drag to rotate.

We usually draw molecules flat because it's quicker and easier to see. Remember that molecules such as CH4, CCl4 and CH2Cl2 are really tetrahedral, and NH3 is pyramidal. Use "Real 3D shape" to check, especially the CH2Cl2 trap.

Section 5

Common mistakes

Missing the symmetry word

"CO2 is non-polar because the dipoles cancel."

"The C=O bonds are polar, but CO2 is linear and symmetrical, so the bond dipoles cancel."

Only giving one condition

"Water is polar because O is more electronegative than H."

"The O–H bonds are polar and the molecule is bent (unsymmetrical), so the dipoles don't cancel."

Calling C–H polar

"Methane is polar because C is more electronegative than H."

"The electronegativity difference between C and H is too small, so C–H bonds are non-polar."

Trusting a flat drawing

"CH2Cl2 drawn with the Cl atoms opposite looks symmetrical, so it's non-polar."

"CH2Cl2 is tetrahedral. The two C–Cl dipoles can never point in opposite directions, so it is polar."

"Polar bonds means a polar molecule"

"CCl4 has polar C–Cl bonds, so it is polar."

"CCl4 is symmetrical (tetrahedral), so the four bond dipoles cancel. No permanent dipole."

Using full charges

"H+–Cl−"

"Hδ+–Clδ−": partial charges, electrons still shared.
