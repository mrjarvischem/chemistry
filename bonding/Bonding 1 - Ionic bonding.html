Section 1

Ionic bonding starts with electron transfer

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms. Nothing is shared.

The metal atom loses its outer electron(s) and becomes a positive ion (a cation). The non-metal atom gains electrons and becomes a negative ion (an anion). Both ions usually end up with the same electron configuration as a noble gas.

Dot-and-cross: watch the transfer

Only the outer shells are drawn. Metal electrons are dots (•), non-metal electrons are crosses (×). The dots and crosses are identical electrons; the symbols just let you track where each one came from.

The ionic bond is not the transfer. Transfer makes the ions. The bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Use exactly that phrase in exams.

Section 3

Compound ions you must know

Some ions contain more than one atom, covalently bonded together, with an overall charge spread over the whole group. You need to learn these six. Their formulae cannot be worked out from the periodic table.

The six
Patterns that help. Sulfate, nitrate and carbonate end in -ate and contain oxygen. Hydrogencarbonate is carbonate with an H+ added, so its charge drops from 2− to 1−. Ammonium is the only positive one.
Section 4

Transition metal ions and Roman numerals

Transition metals can form ions with different charges. Iron, for example, forms both Fe2+ and Fe3+. So the name has to tell you which ion is present. A Roman numeral in brackets after the metal gives the charge on the ion.

Working backwards: formula to name
  1. Take FeCl3. Each chloride is Cl−, so three of them total 3−.
  2. The compound is neutral, so the single iron ion must be 3+.
  3. Name: iron(III) chloride.
The numeral is the charge, not a count. Copper(II) oxide is CuO: one Cu2+ and one O2−. The "II" does not mean two copper atoms. (Later you'll meet this number as the oxidation state.) Metals with only one common ion, such as zinc (Zn2+) and silver (Ag+), don't need a numeral.
Section 5

Working out formulae of ionic compounds

An ionic compound has no overall charge. The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, using the lowest whole-number ratio of ions.

Worked example: aluminium sulfate
  1. Write the ions: Al3+ and SO42−.
  2. Find the lowest common multiple of 3 and 2: 6.
  3. 2 × Al3+ = 6+ and 3 × SO42− = 6−. Balanced.
  4. Write Al2(SO4)3. Brackets go round a compound ion only when you need more than one of it.
Charge-balance builder

Each block's width is proportional to its charge. Add ions until the two rows are the same length.

Brackets matter. Ca(OH)2 means one Ca2+ and two OH−. CaOH2 would mean one O and two H, which is wrong.
Section 6

The giant ionic lattice

Once the ions form, they don't pair off. Every positive ion attracts every negative ion around it. Millions of ions pack into a regular 3D arrangement called a giant ionic lattice.

In sodium chloride, each Na+ is surrounded by six Cl− ions, and each Cl− is surrounded by six Na+ ions. The electrostatic forces act in all directions throughout the lattice.

Sodium chloride lattice · drag to rotate

● Na⁺   ● Cl⁻   The highlighted lines show the attractions between the central Na+ and its six nearest Cl− neighbours. Switch to "All attractions" to see that every ion is held the same way. Cl− is drawn larger because it really is larger: it has an extra shell of electrons.

"NaCl" is a ratio, not a molecule. The formula tells you there is one Na+ for every Cl− in the lattice. Never write "NaCl molecules" or "intermolecular forces" for an ionic compound.
Section 7

Properties come from the structure

High melting and boiling points

Melting means breaking up the lattice. There are many strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome them.

Bigger charges and smaller ions give stronger attraction. MgO (Mg2+, O2−) melts at about 2850 °C; NaCl (Na+, Cl−) melts at 801 °C.

Electrical conductivity

To conduct, charged particles must be free to move. In an ionic compound the charge carriers are the ions, not electrons.

Conductivity test

Brittle

Hit the lattice

A force shifts one layer by a single ion. Now ions with the same charge sit next to each other, they repel, and the crystal splits apart.

Many ionic compounds dissolve in water

Water molecules are polar: the oxygen carries a small negative charge (δ−) and each hydrogen a small positive charge (δ+).

The δ− oxygen atoms are attracted to positive ions. The δ+ hydrogen atoms are attracted to negative ions. Water molecules surround each ion and pull it out of the lattice. The ions are now hydrated, shown by (aq).

Hydrated ions
Not all ionic compounds dissolve. If the attraction between the ions in the lattice is stronger than the attraction to water molecules, the compound is insoluble. Examples: silver chloride (AgCl), barium sulfate (BaSO4) and calcium carbonate (CaCO3).
