Boiling points

More electrons means a larger, more easily distorted electron cloud (higher polarisability). Bigger instantaneous dipoles induce bigger dipoles, so the forces are stronger and more energy is needed to separate the atoms.

What is happening

  1. Electrons are constantly moving within the atom.
  2. At any instant they may be unevenly spread, so one side is slightly negative (δ−) and the other slightly positive (δ+). This is an instantaneous (temporary) dipole.
  3. The δ− side repels electrons in a neighbouring atom (and the δ+ side attracts them), so the neighbour's electron cloud shifts. This is an induced dipole.
  4. The δ+ of one atom attracts the δ− of the next. The dipoles keep flipping, but they flip together, so the atoms always attract.
  5. More electrons → larger dipoles → stronger van der Waals forces → higher boiling point.

Why the branched isomer boils lower

  1. Both isomers have the same formula and the same 66 electrons, so each molecule is equally polarisable.
  2. The straight chain is long and thin, so neighbouring molecules can lie alongside each other with a large surface area in contact.
  3. The branched molecule is compact and closer to spherical. Neighbours touch at fewer points, so fewer induced dipole–dipole attractions form.
  4. Weaker overall van der Waals forces mean less energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point is lower.
