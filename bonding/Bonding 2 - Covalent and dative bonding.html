Section 1

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons

Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms. Each atom puts one electron into the bond, giving a shared pair. Both nuclei are attracted to this shared pair, and that attraction holds the atoms together.

Pairs of outer electrons that are not in a bond are called lone pairs.

Definition. A single covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. The bond is strong because both nuclei are attracted to the same pair.
Section 2

Multiple bonds

Atoms can share more than one pair. A double bond is two shared pairs (4 electrons). A triple bond is three shared pairs (6 electrons).

Section 3

Dative covalent bonds, also called coordinate bonds

In a dative covalent bond (also called a coordinate bond), the shared pair of electrons comes from one atom only. Two things are needed:

The donor

An atom with a lone pair to give.

  • Group 5: N in NH3, P in PH3 (one lone pair)
  • Group 6: O in H2O (two lone pairs)
  • Halide ions such as Cl− (four lone pairs)
The acceptor

A species that is electron deficient: it has room for another pair of electrons in its outer shell.

  • H+: no electrons at all
  • AlCl3 and GaCl3: only 6 outer electrons on Al or Ga
  • BF3: only 6 outer electrons on B
Aluminium chloride dimer, Al₂Cl₆

AlCl3 is electron deficient. At low temperatures two molecules join: a lone pair on a Cl atom of each molecule forms a dative bond to the Al atom of the other. Each Al now has 8 outer electrons.

Once formed, a dative bond is just a covalent bond. In NH4+ all four N–H bonds are identical in length and strength. You can't tell which one was dative. The diagram only records where the electrons came from.

Section 5

Properties of simple molecular substances

Substances such as Cl2, H2O, CH4 and CO2 are made of separate molecules. There are two kinds of attraction to keep apart:

Inside each molecule

Covalent bonds: strong. These are not broken when the substance melts or boils.

Between molecules

Intermolecular forces: weak. These are what you overcome when it melts or boils. You'll meet the different types later in the course.

Low melting and boiling points: only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome, which takes little energy. Many are gases or liquids at room temperature.

Do not conduct electricity: molecules have no overall charge, and there are no ions or delocalised electrons free to move.

Classic mistake. "When water boils, the O–H bonds break." No: the molecules separate but stay intact. Steam is still H2O.

Giant covalent structures such as diamond and graphite behave very differently. They are covered separately.
