Metallic bonding

Section 1

From atoms to delocalised electrons

Metal atoms hold their outer electrons only weakly. In a solid metal the atoms are packed closely together, and the outer electrons are no longer tied to any one atom. They are delocalised: free to move through the whole structure.

Each atom that has lost its outer electrons is left as a positive ion. Work through the three steps below.

Step through the model

Definition. Metallic bonding is the attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice.
Section 2

The lattice in three dimensions

In a real metal the positive ions form a regular 3D lattice, and the delocalised electrons move through all of it. Each ion is attracted to the electrons around it in every direction, and the electrons are attracted to all the ions near them.

Metal lattice · drag to rotate

The ions are shown in a simple cubic arrangement to keep the model clear. Most real metals pack their ions even more closely.

Section 3

Properties of metals

Good electrical conductors

The delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice. When a potential difference is applied, they flow towards the positive terminal and carry charge. This works in the solid and the liquid.

Electrons in a wire

Good thermal conductors

The delocalised electrons pick up kinetic energy and move quickly through the structure, passing energy on as they go.

High melting and boiling points

Most metals have high melting points because the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is strong, and a lot of energy is needed to overcome it.

The attraction gets stronger when the ion has a bigger charge, there are more delocalised electrons per atom, and the ion is smaller.

Malleable and ductile

Hit the metal

The layers of ions slide over each other. The delocalised electrons move with them, so every ion is still surrounded by electrons and the bonding is not broken. Compare an ionic lattice, where sliding puts like charges together and it shatters.

Insoluble

Metals don't dissolve in water or other solvents. Some react with water instead, but that is a chemical change, not dissolving.
