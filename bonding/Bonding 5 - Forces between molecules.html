Section 1

Three forces between molecules

Intermolecular forces act between molecules. They are much weaker than the covalent bonds inside molecules. When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, it's these forces that are overcome, never the covalent bonds.

Van der Waalsinduced dipole–dipole

Between all molecules. Strength depends on the number of electrons.

Permanent dipole–dipole

Between polar molecules, in addition to van der Waals.

Hydrogen bonding

The strongest. Needs H bonded directly to N, O or F.

The bars compare one interaction of each type between small molecules of similar size. A big molecule can have more van der Waals attraction in total than a small one has hydrogen bonding. Section 6 explains why.

Section 2

Van der Waals forces

AQA also calls these induced dipole–dipole forces. They act between all atoms and molecules, polar or not.

How they arise

More electrons (a bigger molecule) means larger temporary dipoles and stronger van der Waals forces. Long, straight molecules have more surface contact than branched ones, so their van der Waals forces are stronger too.

Permanent dipole–dipole forces

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole (Run-through 4). The δ+ end of one molecule is attracted to the δ− end of a neighbouring molecule. These attractions act as well as van der Waals forces.

Section 4

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is a special, much stronger kind of dipole attraction. It happens when a hydrogen atom is bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine.

N, O and F are very electronegative and small, so they pull the electron density strongly away from the H. The H becomes very δ+ and is almost a bare proton. It is strongly attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom in a neighbouring molecule.

Hydrogen bonds between molecules

To draw a hydrogen bond for full marks, show: (1) δ+ on the H and δ− on the N, O or F; (2) the lone pair on the N, O or F of the other molecule; (3) a dashed line from that lone pair to the H. The O–H···O atoms should be in a straight line.
Evidence: boiling points of hydrides

Down each group, boiling points normally rise as the number of electrons increases (stronger van der Waals). Group 4 follows this pattern. NH3, H2O and HF don't: they boil far higher than the trend predicts because their molecules hydrogen bond.

The hydrogen must be bonded directly to N, O or F. Methoxymethane, CH3OCH3, contains O and H, but every H is bonded to C, so its molecules can't hydrogen bond with each other.

Section 6

Boiling points and solubility

The boiling point depends on the total strength of all the forces between the molecules. A polar molecule has a van der Waals component and a permanent dipole (or hydrogen bonding) component. In bigger molecules the van der Waals component can be the more important one.

Same size, different forces

SubstanceMrStrongest forceBoiling point
butane, CH3CH2CH2CH358van der Waals only−1 °C
propanone, CH3COCH358permanent dipole–dipole56 °C
propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH60hydrogen bonding97 °C

With similar numbers of electrons, the van der Waals forces are similar, so the extra force decides the order.

When van der Waals wins

HCl is more polar than HI, yet HI boils higher (HCl −85 °C, HI −35 °C). HI has far more electrons (54 against 18), so its van der Waals forces are much stronger, and that outweighs the bigger permanent dipole in HCl.

Solubility in water

Solubilities are approximate, in g per 100 g of water at 20 °C.

Ethanol

Both ethanol and hexan-1-ol have one O–H group, so both hydrogen bond. Ethanol's chain is short, so hydrogen bonding is a large part of its attraction. Ethanol molecules hydrogen bond to water molecules, so ethanol mixes with water in any proportion.

Hexan-1-ol

Hexan-1-ol has more electrons, so stronger van der Waals forces and a higher boiling point. But its long non-polar chain can't hydrogen bond to water. The van der Waals component now dominates, and hexan-1-ol is only slightly soluble.

Bringing it together. To explain a boiling point: name the forces between the molecules, say which is stronger, and say that more energy is needed to overcome stronger forces. Never say covalent bonds break.
