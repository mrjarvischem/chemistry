Lesson 6 · Refraction, total internal reflection and optical fibres

AQA A-level Physics 3.3.2.2 · about 25 minutes

Refractive index and Snell’s law

Light slows down when it enters a denser optical medium and changes direction (refracts) unless it hits the boundary along the normal.

refractive index n = c / cs   (cs = speed in the substance; n of air ≈ 1)
Snell’s law: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2   (angles measured from the normal)

Angle of incidence: °
When light enters a slower medium its speed and wavelength decrease, but its frequency stays the same (the source decides the frequency).

Critical angle and total internal reflection

Set the top material to glass and the bottom to air in the simulator above (on the previous tab), or use the one below, then increase the angle.

Angle of incidence (glass → air): °
At the critical angle θc the refracted ray travels along the boundary (θ2 = 90°): sin θc = n2/n1.
Total internal reflection happens when light travels into a medium with a lower refractive index and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Step-index optical fibres

An optical fibre is a thin glass core surrounded by cladding of slightly lower refractive index. Light travels along the core by repeated total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary.

Why the cladding?

  • Protects the core from scratches, which would let light escape
  • Stops signals crossing between touching fibres (crossover) — better security
  • Keeps a lower-n layer so TIR can happen

Signal problems

  • Absorption: energy is absorbed by the core, so the signal amplitude falls → use repeaters/amplifiers.
  • Material dispersion: different wavelengths travel at different speeds in glass → pulse broadening → use monochromatic light.
  • Modal dispersion: rays at different angles travel different path lengths → arrive at different times → pulse broadening → use a very narrow core (single-mode fibre).

Pulse broadening

Fibre length: km
Pulse broadening matters because broadened pulses overlap, so the receiver can’t tell them apart. That limits how close together pulses can be sent — the maximum rate of data transmission (bandwidth).

Exam practice

c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1.

Lesson summary — and the end of section 3.3!

  • n = c/cs; n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Speed and wavelength change; frequency doesn’t.
  • sin θc = n₂/n₁. TIR: into lower n, and θ > θc.
  • Fibres: core (higher n) + cladding (lower n). Cladding protects, prevents crossover.
  • Absorption → amplitude falls. Material dispersion (fix: monochromatic) and modal dispersion (fix: narrow core) → pulse broadening, limiting data rate.
