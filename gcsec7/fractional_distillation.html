AQA GCSE Chemistry 4.7.1.2–4.7.1.3 · Combined Science 5.7.1.2–5.7.1.3 · about 30 minutes

Fractional distillation

The hydrocarbons in crude oil are separated into fractions by fractional distillation. Each fraction contains molecules with a similar number of carbon atoms, so they have similar boiling points. Tap a fraction.

How it works

  1. Crude oil is heated so that most of it evaporates.
  2. The vapour goes into the column, which is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top.
  3. The vapours rise. Each fraction condenses when it reaches the level where the temperature is below its boiling point.
  4. Long-chain molecules (high boiling points) condense near the bottom; short chains rise to the top. Gases come out of the top.
Fractions are processed to make fuels and feedstock for the petrochemical industry — e.g. solvents, lubricants, polymers and detergents. Many useful materials are made from crude oil because carbon atoms can form families of similar compounds.

How chain length affects properties

As chain length increases…
boiling pointincreases
viscosity (how thick / slow-flowing)increases
flammability (how easily it ignites)decreases

Shorter chains make better fuels: they are runny, easy to ignite and burn cleanly.

Why does boiling point increase with chain length? Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces between them (there is more contact between the molecules). More energy is needed to overcome these forces, so the boiling point is higher.
When a hydrocarbon boils, the intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome. The covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken.

Complete combustion

When hydrocarbon fuels burn, energy is released. During complete combustion the carbon and hydrogen atoms are oxidised.

hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Balance in this order: C first (CO₂), then H (H₂O), then O last. If you need an odd number of O atoms, double everything.

Quiz and exam practice

Summary

  • Fractional distillation: heat to evaporate; column hot at bottom, cool at top; fractions condense at their boiling points.
  • Fractions: refinery gases, petrol, kerosene, diesel oil, heavy fuel oil, bitumen — used as fuels and feedstock.
  • Longer chain → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed to overcome them → higher boiling point. Also more viscous, less flammable.
  • Complete combustion: hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
