Walkthrough 3 · Cracking and alkenes

AQA GCSE Chemistry 4.7.1.4 · Combined Science 5.7.1.4 · about 30 minutes

Why are hydrocarbons cracked?

Crude oil contains more long-chain hydrocarbons than we need, and not enough short-chain ones. Cracking breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful molecules.

Cracking produces alkanes (short chains — useful as fuels, e.g. petrol) and alkenes (used to make polymers and as a starting material for many other chemicals).

Two ways to crack

Catalytic cracking

Heat the long-chain hydrocarbon to vaporise it, then pass the vapour over a hot catalyst.

Steam cracking

Mix the hydrocarbon vapour with steam and heat to a very high temperature.
In the lab, liquid paraffin on mineral wool is heated; its vapour passes over hot broken porcelain (or aluminium oxide) as a catalyst. The gas is collected over water.

Balancing cracking equations

No atoms are lost: the number of C atoms and H atoms on the left equals the total on the right. Find the missing formula by subtracting.

Example: {C10H22 -> C8H18 + ?}. Carbon: 10 − 8 = 2. Hydrogen: 22 − 18 = 4. So the missing product is {C2H4} (ethene — an alkene).

Testing for alkenes: bromine water

Alkenes are more reactive than alkanes. They react with bromine water, which is orange.

Alkene + orange bromine water → colourless. Alkane: bromine water stays orange.
Say colourless, not “clear”. Clear means see-through (orange bromine water is already clear).

Quiz and exam practice

Summary

  • Cracking breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter alkanes and alkenes.
  • Catalytic cracking: vapour over a hot catalyst. Steam cracking: vapour + steam at very high temperature.
  • Balance cracking equations by subtracting C and H atoms.
  • Alkenes turn orange bromine water colourless; alkanes don't.
  • Alkenes make polymers; short alkanes make fuels. Supply and demand drive cracking.
