Section 1

Why do we need a reference?

A voltmeter measures a potential difference: it needs two electrodes. So you can never measure the potential of one half cell on its own. You can only measure it compared with another half cell.

The answer is to pick one half cell, give it the value 0.00 V, and measure everything else against it. It's like measuring the heights of mountains from sea level: sea level isn't really "zero height", but agreeing on it lets every height be compared.

Every E⦵ is measured from the same zero
The reference is the standard hydrogen electrode. Its potential is defined as 0.00 V. Half cells above it on the scale have positive E⦵; those below have negative E⦵.
Section 2

The standard hydrogen electrode

The standard hydrogen electrode

Components

  • Hydrogen gas at 100 kPa, bubbled in
  • 1.00 mol dm−3 H+(aq), e.g. HCl(aq)
  • Platinum electrode, coated in platinum black
  • At 298 K
2H+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ H2(g)  E⦵ = 0.00 V

Why platinum?

Neither H2 nor H+ is a solid conductor, so an inert electrode is needed. Platinum conducts and doesn't react.

Why platinum black?

A finely divided coating gives a large surface area, so the gas, ions and electrode reach equilibrium quickly.

Why these conditions?

It is an equilibrium. Changing the H2 pressure, [H+] or temperature would shift it and change the potential, so it would no longer be 0.00 V.

Going further: why labs don't use it every day

A hydrogen electrode is awkward: it needs a steady supply of pure hydrogen at exactly 100 kPa, and the platinum surface is easily “poisoned” by impurities. In practice, chemists use a secondary reference electrode, such as a silver/silver chloride electrode, whose potential has been measured accurately against the hydrogen electrode.

Section 3

Measuring a standard electrode potential

The hydrogen electrode always goes on the left. Then Ecell = Eright − 0.00 = Eright, so the voltmeter reads the E⦵ of the other half cell directly, sign and all.
Choose the half cell to measure

Positive reading

The half cell is the positive electrode. Its oxidised form is reduced more easily than H+. Electrons flow from the hydrogen electrode to it. Cu2+/Cu: +0.34 V.

Negative reading

The half cell is the negative electrode. Its reduced form is oxidised more easily than H2. Electrons flow from it to the hydrogen electrode. Zn2+/Zn: −0.76 V.

To get the standard electrode potential, both half cells must be under standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm−3 solutions.
Section 4

Cell representations with the hydrogen electrode

The hydrogen electrode is a gas electrode, so platinum goes at the far end, and lines separate the solid, the gas and the solution. On the left it is written in the oxidation direction: H2 first, then H+.

Pt(s) | H2(g) | H+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)   Ecell = +0.34 V
Pt(s) | H2(g) | H+(aq) || Fe3+(aq), Fe2+(aq) | Pt(s)   Ecell = +0.77 V

When measuring E⦵, the hydrogen electrode stays on the left even if the other half cell is more negative. The emf then comes out negative, which is exactly the E⦵ you want: Pt(s) | H2(g) | H+(aq) || Zn2+(aq) | Zn(s) gives −0.76 V.
