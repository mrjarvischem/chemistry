The Ideal Gas Equation – pV = nRT

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.1.2 Amount of substance walkthrough · R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

1. The ideal gas equation

pV = nRT
SymbolQuantityUnit you MUST use
ppressurePa (pascal)
Vvolumem³
namount of substancemol
Rgas constant = 8.31J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
TtemperatureK (kelvin)

R is a constant, so it only works if every other quantity is in these SI units. Getting the units wrong is the most common way to lose marks.

See the equation in action

The particles of gas hit the walls of the container. Those collisions cause the pressure. Change the amount, temperature or volume and watch what happens to the pressure.

What the simulation shows:
  • More moles → more particles hitting the walls → higher pressure (p ∝ n)
  • Higher temperature → particles move faster and hit harder and more often → higher pressure (p ∝ T)
  • Bigger volume → particles hit the walls less often → lower pressure (p ∝ 1/V)

2. Rearranging pV = nRT

You are usually asked to find one quantity. Rearrange before you put the numbers in. To move a term that is multiplying, divide both sides by it.

To find…Use
pp = nRTV
VV = nRTp
nn = pVRT
TT = pVnR

Watch it rearrange

Pick the quantity you want to find.

Memory trick: the top of the fraction is always the other side of the equation from the quantity you want. pV is on the left, nRT is on the right. If you want something from the right (n or T), pV goes on top. If you want something from the left (p or V), nRT goes on top.

Why kelvin matters

If you forget to convert °C into K, your answer can be wrong by a factor of more than 10. Compare the right and wrong answers for n = pV / RT.

3. Unit conversions

Temperature: °C → K

T (K) = T (°C) + 273

The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C), the coldest possible temperature. A change of 1 K is the same size as a change of 1 °C.

Volume: cm³ → dm³ → m³

1 m³ = 1000 dm³ = 1 000 000 cm³

Each step on the ladder is × 1000 or ÷ 1000. Going to a bigger unit (cm³ → m³), the number gets smaller, so divide.

Type a volume:

Shortcuts: cm³ → m³ is × 10⁻⁶  ·  dm³ → m³ is × 10⁻³

Pressure: kPa → Pa

1 kPa = 1000 Pa   ·   1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa

So 101 kPa = 101 000 Pa = 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa.

Conversion drill

Practise until it's automatic. You get a new question each time. Use standard form if you like (type 2.5e-4 for 2.5 × 10⁻⁴).

4. Using pV = nRT

The method – the same every time:

  1. Write down the values you are given.
  2. Convert them to Pa, m³ and K.
  3. Rearrange pV = nRT for the quantity you want.
  4. Substitute and calculate.
  5. Convert the answer to the unit the question asks for, and give it to 3 significant figures.

AQA mark schemes usually give separate marks for the rearranged equation, for the unit conversions and for the final answer.

Worked example 1 – finding n

Calculate the amount, in moles, of gas in 1.50 dm³ at 98.0 kPa and 20 °C.

p = 98.0 kPa = 98 000 Pa  ·  V = 1.50 dm³ = 1.50 × 10⁻³ m³  ·  T = 20 + 273 = 293 KConvert everything first.
n = pVRTRearrange.
n = 98 000 × 1.50 × 10⁻³8.31 × 293 = 1472434.8Substitute. Work out the top and bottom separately to avoid calculator errors.
n = 0.06037… = 0.0604 mol3 significant figures.

Worked example 2 – finding V in cm³

Calculate the volume, in cm³, of 0.0200 mol of gas at 100 kPa and 300 K.

p = 100 000 Pa  ·  T = 300 K  ·  n = 0.0200 mol
V = nRTp = 0.0200 × 8.31 × 300100 000 = 4.986 × 10⁻⁴ m³The equation always gives V in m³.
4.986 × 10⁻⁴ × 10⁶ = 499 cm³m³ → cm³: multiply by 10⁶. Check it's sensible: a small amount of gas, a few hundred cm³ ✓

Worked example 3 – finding T

AQA AS Paper 1, June 2021 (sat November 2021), Q10.3

A mixture contains 0.025 mol of SO₂, 0.049 mol of O₂ and 0.034 mol of SO₃. The total pressure of the mixture in a 3500 cm³ vessel is 255 kPa. Calculate the temperature, in °C, of the mixture. [5 marks]

T = pVnRM1: rearranged expression.
n = 0.025 + 0.049 + 0.034 = 0.108 molM2: the pressure is caused by ALL the gas particles, so use the total amount.
p = 255 000 Pa  ·  V = 3500 cm³ = 0.0035 m³M3: unit conversions.
T = 255 000 × 0.00358.31 × 0.108 = 994.5 KM4: temperature in K.
994.5 − 273 = 721 °CM5: the question asked for °C.

5. Mass and relative molecular mass

pV = nRT gives you moles. Combine it with the mole equation to link it to mass:

n = mMr   so   mass = n × Mr   and   Mr = massn

The two-step method is safest: (1) find n using pV = nRT, (2) use n with the mass. You can also combine them into one equation:

Mr = mRTpV

Type 1 – find the mass of a gas

Calculate the mass, in g, of 750 cm³ of oxygen gas, O₂, at 100 kPa and 300 K.

n = pVRT = 100 000 × 7.50 × 10⁻⁴8.31 × 300 = 0.03008 mol750 cm³ = 7.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³
Mr(O₂) = 2 × 16.0 = 32.0
mass = n × Mr = 0.03008 × 32.0 = 0.963 g

Type 2 – find Mr of a volatile liquid

This is a very common exam experiment. A small mass of a volatile liquid is injected into a gas syringe inside an oven. The liquid turns into a gas, and the plunger moves out. The mass comes from weighing the small syringe before and after injecting.

AQA AS Paper 2, June 2025, Q05.2

Liquid X is vaporised in a gas syringe. The data are: mass of syringe + X before = 106.633 g, after = 105.872 g; volume of vapour = 272 cm³; temperature = 373 K; pressure = 102 kPa. Calculate the Mr of X. [5 marks]

n = pVRTM1
V = 272 × 10⁻⁶ m³  ·  p = 102 000 Pa  ·  T = 373 KM2: unit conversions.
n = 102 000 × 272 × 10⁻⁶8.31 × 373 = 0.00895 molM3
mass of X = 106.633 − 105.872 = 0.761 gM4: mass by difference.
Mr = 0.7610.00895 = 85.0M5. Mr has no units.

Type 3 – identify the compound

Once you have Mr, compare it with the possible compounds. For an alkane, CnH2n+2:

Mr = 12n + 1(2n + 2) = 14n + 2

e.g. Mr = 72.0 → 14n + 2 = 72 → n = 5 → C₅H₁₂ (pentane)
