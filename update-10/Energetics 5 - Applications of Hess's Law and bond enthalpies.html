Applications of Hess's Law and bond enthalpies

Cycles built from experiments, mean bond enthalpy calculations, and why different methods give different answers.

Using Hess's law with your own experiments

Some enthalpy changes cannot be measured directly:

Side reactions

C + ½O₂ → CO

Some CO₂ always forms too.

Too hot to measure

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Needs strong, continuous heating, so there is no temperature change to read.

Does not happen

3C + 4H₂ → C₃H₈

The elements do not combine like this.

For calcium carbonate there is a way round. Both CaCO₃ and CaO react with hydrochloric acid in a polystyrene cup, and both end up as the same solution. Those two enthalpy changes can be measured by calorimetry.

1
The target: the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. Add 2HCl to both sides so both can react.
2
Both sides react with the acid to give the same products, so the arrows point down to CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
3
The values measured by calorimetry: CaCO₃ + 2HCl, ΔH₁ = −13.7; CaO + 2HCl, ΔH₂ = −153.3 kJ mol⁻¹.
4
Reactants to products the long way: with the left arrow, then against the right arrow.
5
ΔH = −13.7 + 153.3 = +139.6 kJ mol⁻¹. Positive, so endothermic, as expected for a thermal decomposition that needs constant heating.

CHECK 1

Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate cannot be hydrated in a controlled way, so ΔH for CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) is found indirectly. Dissolving CuSO₄(s) in water gives ΔH = −66 kJ mol⁻¹. Dissolving CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) in water gives ΔH = +12 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH for the hydration.

Both dissolve to give the same solution, so both arrows point down to CuSO₄(aq).
With the left arrow, against the right: ΔH = −66 − (+12) = −78 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 2

Explain why the enthalpy of combustion of oxygen is taken as zero in a combustion cycle.

Oxygen is what things burn in. It cannot itself be burned, so no enthalpy change is involved.

Enthalpy changes from bond enthalpies

Any reaction can be imagined in two stages: break every bond in the reactants to get separate gaseous atoms, then make every bond in the products. This is another Hess cycle, with the atoms at the top.

Breaking bonds

Takes energy in: endothermic, +.

Making bonds

Gives energy out: exothermic, −.

The calculation

ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds made)

Mean bond enthalpies used in this walkthrough / kJ mol⁻¹

Worked example: burning methane

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)

1
Draw every molecule out so you can see all the bonds:   CH₄ has 4 C–H; each O₂ has 1 O=O; CO₂ is O=C=O with 2 C=O; each H₂O has 2 O–H.
2
Bonds broken (reactants): 4 × C–H + 2 × O=O = (4 × 413) + (2 × 498) = 1652 + 996 = 2648
3
Bonds made (products): 2 × C=O + 4 × O–H = (2 × 805) + (4 × 464) = 1610 + 1856 = 3466
4
ΔH = broken − made = 2648 − 3466

ΔH = −818 kJ mol⁻¹

5
More energy is given out making bonds than is taken in breaking them, so the reaction is exothermic.
Count the bonds, not the molecules. The most common mistakes are forgetting to multiply by the number in front (2O₂, 2H₂O) and forgetting that one H₂O has two O–H bonds.

CHECK 3

Calculate ΔH for C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g).
Mean bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: C=C 612, C–C 347, C–H 413, H–H 436

Broken: C=C + H–H = 612 + 436 = 1048 (the four C–H bonds in ethene are unchanged, so they can be left out)
Made: C–C + 2 × C–H = 347 + 826 = 1173
ΔH = 1048 − 1173 = −125 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 4

Calculate ΔH for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g).
Mean bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: N≡N 945, H–H 436, N–H 391

Broken: 945 + (3 × 436) = 2253
Made: 2 NH₃ contain 6 N–H bonds: 6 × 391 = 2346
ΔH = 2253 − 2346 = −93 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 5

Calculate ΔH for C₂H₅OH(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(g).
Mean bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: C–H 413, C–C 347, C–O 358, O–H 464, O=O 498, C=O 805

Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, has 5 C–H, 1 C–C, 1 C–O and 1 O–H.
Broken: (5 × 413) + 347 + 358 + 464 + (3 × 498) = 2065 + 347 + 358 + 464 + 1494 = 4728
Made: (4 × 805) + (6 × 464) = 3220 + 2784 = 6004
ΔH = 4728 − 6004 = −1276 kJ mol⁻¹

Working backwards: finding a bond enthalpy

If you are given ΔH, the same equation can be rearranged to find one unknown bond enthalpy. Call it x.

Worked example

H₂(g) + F₂(g) → 2HF(g)   ΔH = −542 kJ mol⁻¹. Bond enthalpies: H–H 436, F–F 158 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the H–F bond enthalpy.

1
Bonds broken: 436 + 158 = 594
2
Bonds made: 2 × H–F = 2x
3
ΔH = broken − made:   −542 = 594 − 2x
4
2x = 594 + 542 = 1136

x = +568 kJ mol⁻¹

A bond enthalpy is always positive. If you get a negative answer, the rearranging has gone wrong.

CHECK 6

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g)   ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. Bond enthalpies: N≡N 945, H–H 436 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the mean N–H bond enthalpy.

Broken: 945 + (3 × 436) = 2253   Made: 6x
−92 = 2253 − 6x, so 6x = 2345 and x = +391 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 7

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g)   ΔH = −486 kJ mol⁻¹. Bond enthalpies: H–H 436, O–H 464 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the O=O bond enthalpy.

Broken: (2 × 436) + x = 872 + x   Made: 4 × 464 = 1856
−486 = 872 + x − 1856, so x = −486 + 984 = +498 kJ mol⁻¹

Three methods, three answers

The enthalpy of combustion of ethanol found three different ways:

Why is the bond enthalpy value different?

  • Mean bond enthalpies are averaged over many compounds. They are not the exact values for the bonds in ethanol, CO₂ and water.
  • Bond enthalpies apply to gases. In the standard enthalpy of combustion, ethanol and water are liquids, so the energy for the changes of state is missing.

Why is the calorimetry value so far out?

  • Heat is lost to the air and the calorimeter.
  • Combustion is incomplete.
  • Some fuel evaporates.

See Walkthrough 3 for how each one affects the calculation.

CHECK 8

A Hess cycle using data-book ΔfH values gives ΔH = −137 kJ mol⁻¹ for C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆. Mean bond enthalpies give −125 kJ mol⁻¹. Which is more accurate, and why?

−137. The ΔfH values are measured for those exact substances. Mean bond enthalpies are averages over a range of compounds, so they only give an approximate answer.

CHECK 9

For H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g), the bond enthalpy answer (−185) is almost identical to the data-book value (−184). Suggest why it works so well here.

H–H, Cl–Cl and H–Cl bonds each occur in only one molecule, so the values are not averages over different compounds. Everything is also a gas.
