Rate Equations 1 – Measuring and Calculating Rate

AQA A-level Chemistry · Kinetics walkthrough

1. What is rate of reaction?

The rate of reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

rate = change in concentrationtime taken = Δ[A]Δt

Square brackets mean "concentration of", so [A] is the concentration of A in mol dm⁻³.

Rate is always written as a positive number. A reactant's concentration goes down, so we take the size of the change and ignore the minus sign.

Units of rate

The units come straight from the calculation: the units of whatever you measured, divided by the units of time.

mol dm⁻³ ÷ s = mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

If you measured something other than concentration, the units change to match it. Try it:

Exam tip: write units with negative indices, e.g. cm³ s⁻¹. AQA mark schemes have refused "cm³/s" (June 2020 Paper 2, Q1.1).

Worked example

Substance X has an initial concentration of 1.00 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³. After 5 seconds the concentration is 0.93 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³. Calculate the initial rate and give units.

  1. Change in concentration = 1.00 × 10⁻² − 0.93 × 10⁻² = 0.07 × 10⁻² = 7.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³
  2. Rate = 7.0 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 5 = 1.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  3. We can call this the initial rate because 5 s is a very short time near the start. Over that time the rate has barely changed, so the average rate is very close to the rate at t = 0.

Δ[HCl] = 0.500 − 0.421 = 0.079 mol dm⁻³

QUICK CHECK 2 – working backwards

The initial rate of a reaction is 0.046 mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. The initial concentration of A is 0.600 mol dm⁻³. Assuming the rate stays the same, calculate [A] after 5 seconds.

Concentration used up = rate × time = 0.046 × 5 = 0.23 mol dm⁻³
[A] = 0.600 − 0.23 = 0.37 mol dm⁻³

rate = 1.49 × 10⁻² ÷ 5.0 = 2.98 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

2. Finding the initial rate from a graph

On a concentration–time graph, the rate at any moment is the gradient of the curve at that time.

The initial rate is the gradient of the tangent drawn at t = 0.

  1. Draw a straight line that just touches the curve at t = 0. It should match the curve's slope there, not cut across it.
  2. Extend the tangent as far as you can (for example down to the time axis) to make a big triangle. A bigger triangle gives a more accurate gradient.
  3. Gradient = Δy ÷ Δx. Read the values from the axes carefully.
  4. Rate = size of the gradient. Drop the minus sign for a falling reactant curve.

Interactive: tangents along the curve

Drag the slider to move the tangent point. Watch the gradient get less steep as the reaction goes on.

Common mistakes
• Drawing a chord (a line joining two points on the curve) instead of a tangent. This gives an average rate, not the initial rate.
• Using a tiny triangle. Small reading errors then make a big difference to the answer.
• Giving a negative rate.
• For a product curve (one that rises), use the same method. The gradient is positive.

3. How the rate changes during a reaction

For this walkthrough we look only at first order reactions, where rate is directly proportional to [A].

Animation: reactant particles being used up

● reactant   ● product   ✸ successful collision

Describe and explain how the rate changes over time

The kind of answer the mark scheme is looking for:

  1. Describe: the rate is fastest at the start and decreases over time. The curve gets less steep and levels off when the reaction finishes.
  2. Reactant particles are used up, so the concentration of reactant decreases. There are fewer particles in the same volume.
  3. So there are fewer collisions per unit time (collisions become less frequent).
  4. So there are fewer successful collisions per unit time, meaning collisions with energy ≥ Ea. The rate decreases.
Don't write "fewer collisions". Write "fewer collisions per unit time" or "less frequent collisions". It's the rate of collisions that matters, not the total number.

First order: rate ∝ [A]

For a first order reaction, rate = k[A]. When [A] halves, the rate halves. So the curve keeps getting less steep in a very regular way.

This gives a first order reaction a constant half-life: it always takes the same time for [A] to halve, whatever concentration you start from.

t / s[A] / mol dm⁻³rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

rate = k[A] with k = 0.020 s⁻¹. Each half-life ≈ 35 s.

4. Clock reactions: why rate ∝ 1/t

In a clock reaction you don't follow the whole reaction. Instead you measure the time, t, taken for a fixed amount of product to form. A sudden, visible change tells you when that fixed amount has been made.

  • Iodine clock: a small, fixed amount of thiosulfate removes iodine as fast as it forms. When the thiosulfate runs out, free iodine reacts with starch and the mixture turns blue-black.
  • Disappearing cross: Na₂S₂O₃ + HCl forms solid sulfur. When a fixed amount of sulfur has formed, the cross under the flask can no longer be seen.

Why does 1/t measure the rate?

  1. Rate = amount of product formedtime taken
  2. In every experiment the same fixed amount of product forms before the change is seen. It's set by the amount of thiosulfate, or the amount of sulfur needed to hide the cross.
  3. So rate = constantt, which means rate ∝ 1/t. Halve the time and you double the rate.
  4. This only works as a measure of the initial rate because the fixed amount is small. Only a tiny fraction of the reactants is used up, so their concentrations hardly change during the timing. The average rate over time t is then almost exactly the initial rate.

Interactive: why the fixed amount must be small

The orange line is the tangent at t = 0 (the true initial rate). The blue line is the chord from the start to the moment the fixed amount is reached. 1/t actually measures the chord's gradient.

Worked example

June 2025 Paper 3 Q2.4 (3 marks)

In each experiment, 1.00 cm³ of 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ sodium thiosulfate reacts with the iodine first produced. The total volume in experiment 3 is 40.0 cm³ and the blue-black colour appears after 20 s. Calculate the initial rate of production of iodine in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

  1. n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 1.00 × 10⁻³ × 1.00/1000 = 1.00 × 10⁻⁶ mol
  2. I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2, so n(I₂) formed before the colour change = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol
  3. [I₂] = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ ÷ (40.0/1000) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³
  4. rate = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 20 = 6.25 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
QUICK CHECK 7

Using the same method, calculate the initial rate of production of iodine for experiment 1 (t = 60 s, same thiosulfate, total volume 40.0 cm³).

[I₂] formed = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ (the same fixed amount as before)
rate = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 60 = 2.08 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. That's one-third of experiment 3's rate, because the time is three times longer.
