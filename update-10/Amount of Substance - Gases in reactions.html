The Ideal Gas Equation 2 – Gases in Reactions

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.1.2 Amount of substance walkthrough · R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

1. Volume of gas made in a reaction

pV = nRT only ever deals with moles of gas. In a reaction question you have to get from the substance you know about to the gas, using the balanced equation.

The same map works backwards: volume of gas → moles of gas → ÷ ratio → moles of reactant → mass.

Worked example – going forwards

0.240 g of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume, in cm³, of hydrogen produced at 100 kPa and 298 K.

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
n(Mg) = 0.24024.3 = 9.877 × 10⁻³ molMass → moles of the substance you know about.
Mg : H₂ = 1 : 1  →  n(H₂) = 9.877 × 10⁻³ molUse the equation to get moles of GAS.
V = nRTp = 9.877 × 10⁻³ × 8.31 × 298100 000 = 2.446 × 10⁻⁴ m³
× 10⁶ = 245 cm³

Worked example – going backwards

AQA A-level Paper 2, June 2017, Q10.2

Compound P, C₆H₁₀O₂ (Mr = 114.0), is burned completely in oxygen. 180 cm³ of CO₂ is formed, measured at 105 kPa and 298 K. Calculate the mass, in mg, of P that was burned. [5 marks]

C₆H₁₀O₂ + 7½O₂ → 6CO₂ + 5H₂O
n(CO₂) = 105 000 × 180 × 10⁻⁶8.31 × 298 = 7.632 × 10⁻³ molStart with the gas: volume → moles.
P : CO₂ = 1 : 6  →  n(P) = 7.632 × 10⁻³ ÷ 6 = 1.272 × 10⁻³ molGoing backwards, divide by 6.
mass = 1.272 × 10⁻³ × 114.0 = 0.145 g = 145 mg
Limiting reagent: if amounts of two reactants are given, work out which one runs out first. Use the limiting reactant to calculate the moles of gas.
CHECK 1Practice

Heating potassium chlorate(V) produced 67.2 cm³ of oxygen, measured at 298 K and 110 kPa. Calculate the amount, in moles, of KClO₃ that decomposed.

2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g)

n(KClO₃) = mol

n(O₂) = (110 000 × 67.2 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 2.985 × 10⁻³ mol
KClO₃ : O₂ = 2 : 3 → n(KClO₃) = 2.985 × 10⁻³ × 2/3 = 1.99 × 10⁻³ mol

2. When several gases are produced

Gas particles all behave the same way in pV = nRT – it doesn't matter which gas they are. So if a reaction makes a mixture of gases, the volume (or pressure) depends on the total moles of gas.

  1. Find which products are gases – look for (g). Solids and liquids don't count.
  2. Add up the coefficients of the gases. This gives the total moles of gas made by the equation.
  3. Compare that total with the coefficient of the substance you know about.

Worked example – TNT

AQA AS Paper 2, June 2022, Q04.1

TNT (C₇H₅N₃O₆) decomposes very quickly and exothermically to form a large volume of gas.

2C₇H₅N₃O₆(s) → 3N₂(g) + 5H₂(g) + 12CO(g) + 2C(s)

Calculate the volume of gas, in m³, measured at 1250 °C and 101 000 Pa, produced by the decomposition of 1.00 kg of TNT (Mr = 227.0). [5 marks]

n(TNT) = 1000227.0 = 4.405 molM1: 1.00 kg = 1000 g.
gas: 3 + 5 + 12 = 20 mol of gas per 2 mol TNT → 10 mol gas per mol TNTC(s) is a solid, so it doesn't count.
n(gas) = 10 × 4.405 = 44.05 molM2
V = nRTp  ·  T = 1250 + 273 = 1523 KM3 and M4
V = 44.05 × 8.31 × 1523101 000 = 5.52 m³M5. From 1 kg of solid – that's why it's an explosive!
The mark scheme's common errors: using 4.41 mol (forgetting the total gas) gives 0.552 m³ · using 1250 K gives 4.54 m³ · using 1250 − 273 gives 3.54 m³. Each loses a mark.

3. Working back from a total volume of gas

Many exam questions give the total volume of a gas mixture and ask you to find the Mr of the solid that made it.

total V → total n(gas) → × mol solidtotal mol gas → n(solid) → Mr = massn

AQA A-level Paper 1, June 2024, Q09.4

A metal nitrate X(NO₃)₂ completely decomposes when heated:

2X(NO₃)₂(s) → 2XO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)

A 0.832 g sample of X(NO₃)₂ produces a total of 348 cm³ of gas at 298 K and 100 kPa. Deduce the identity of metal X. [6 marks]

p = 100 000 Pa  ·  V = 348 × 10⁻⁶ m³M1
n(gas) = 100 000 × 348 × 10⁻⁶8.31 × 298 = 0.01405 molM2 and M3. This is the TOTAL – NO₂ and O₂ together.
gas: 4 + 1 = 5 mol per 2 mol X(NO₃)₂ → n(X(NO₃)₂) = 0.01405 × 25 = 5.62 × 10⁻³ molM4
Mr = 0.8325.62 × 10⁻³ = 148.0M5
Ar(X) = 148.0 − (2 × 14.0 + 6 × 16.0) = 148.0 − 124.0 = 24.0 → magnesiumM6. Take off the two NO₃ groups (2 × 62.0).

4. What gas is left after a reaction?

When gases react, the gas left at the end can include:

  • gaseous products (e.g. CO₂)
  • any reactant that was in excess – the leftover doesn't disappear!
  • water – but only if the temperature is high enough for it to be steam. Below 100 °C (at about 100 kPa) it condenses to a liquid and no longer counts.

A before / after table keeps track of everything.

Before and after

Methane burns in excess oxygen: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O. Change the starting amounts and the temperature after the reaction.

Worked example

AQA AS Paper 2, June 2021 (sat November 2021), Q06.2

All the air is removed from a flask. 0.0010 mol of 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (C₈H₁₈) and 0.0200 mol of oxygen are added. The mixture is sparked for complete combustion, then cooled to the original temperature, so the water is a liquid.

C₈H₁₈(g) + 12½O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 9H₂O(l)

Calculate the amount, in moles, of gas in the flask after the reaction. [2 marks]

C₈H₁₈O₂CO₂H₂O(l)
start0.00100.020000
change−0.0010−0.0125+0.0080+0.0090
end00.00750.0080liquid – ignore
Use the equation ratios: 0.0010 × 12.5 = 0.0125 mol of O₂ used; 0.0010 × 8 = 0.0080 mol of CO₂ formed.
n(CO₂) = 0.0080 molM1
total gas = 0.0075 (O₂ left) + 0.0080 (CO₂) = 0.0155 molM2. The excess oxygen is still a gas – don't forget it!
Shortcut when everything is at the same T and p: volume is proportional to moles, so you can use the volumes of gases directly in the equation ratios. You don't need pV = nRT at all.
CHECK 3AQA past paper (Amount of Substance question bank)

600 cm³ of propanone is mixed with 2800 cm³ of oxygen at 60 °C and 100 kPa and ignited. After the reaction, the gases are returned to 60 °C and 100 kPa.

CH₃COCH₃(g) + 4O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

Calculate the total volume of the remaining gas mixture. [2 marks]

V = cm³

O₂ used = 4 × 600 = 2400 cm³ → O₂ left = 2800 − 2400 = 400 cm³
CO₂ formed = 3 × 600 = 1800 cm³ (water is liquid)
Total = 400 + 1800 = 2200 cm³
CHECK 4Practice

0.0100 mol of methane is burned with 0.0300 mol of oxygen (an excess): CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

(a) Calculate the total volume, in dm³, of gas after the reaction at 150 °C and 100 kPa.
V = dm³

(b) The mixture is cooled to 20 °C at the same pressure. Calculate the new volume, in dm³.
V = dm³

After: O₂ left = 0.0300 − 0.0200 = 0.0100 · CO₂ = 0.0100 · H₂O = 0.0200 mol
(a) At 150 °C water is a gas: total = 0.0400 mol. V = (0.0400 × 8.31 × 423) ÷ 100 000 = 1.41 × 10⁻³ m³ = 1.41 dm³
(b) At 20 °C water condenses: total = 0.0200 mol. V = (0.0200 × 8.31 × 293) ÷ 100 000 = 4.87 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = 0.487 dm³

5. Changing the conditions

If a gas is heated or cooled but no gas is added or removed (n stays the same), you can either:

  • use pV = nRT again with the new conditions, or
  • use the shortcut. At constant pressure, V ∝ T (in kelvin):
V₂ = V₁ × T₂T₁    (constant p and n)

Heat and cool a gas syringe

The pressure stays at atmospheric pressure, so the plunger moves to keep it constant.

Doubling the temperature in °C does not double the volume. Only doubling it in kelvin does.

AQA past paper (Amount of Substance question bank)

A sealed gas syringe contains 0.146 g of propanone (Mr = 58.0, boiling point 56.2 °C) at 95 °C and 103 kPa.

(a) Calculate the volume, in cm³, of propanone in the syringe. (b) The syringe is cooled to 75 °C without changing the pressure. Calculate the decrease in volume.

n = 0.146 ÷ 58.0 = 2.517 × 10⁻³ mol  ·  T = 368 K
V = 2.517 × 10⁻³ × 8.31 × 368103 000 = 7.47 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 74.7 cm³
(b) 75 °C = 348 K. Still above the boiling point, so it is still all gas.Always check that nothing condenses!
V₂ = 74.7 × 348368 = 70.7 cm³  →  decrease = 74.7 − 70.7 = 4.1 cm³
CHECK 5Practice

120 cm³ of nitrogen at 27 °C is heated to 177 °C at constant pressure. Calculate the new volume.

V = cm³

T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 450 K → V₂ = 120 × 450/300 = 180 cm³
(Using °C would give 120 × 177/27 = 787 cm³ – way off.)
