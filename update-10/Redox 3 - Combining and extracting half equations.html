Section 1

Electrons lost = electrons gained

An oxidation can't happen on its own. The electrons lost by one species are gained by another. Electrons are never left over, so they never appear in the overall equation.

Chlorine oxidising iron(II) ions
Count them. Each Fe2+ loses 1 electron. Each Cl2 gains 2. So you need 2 Fe2+ for every Cl2: 2Fe2+ + Cl2 → 2Fe3+ + 2Cl−.
Section 2

Combining half equations

① Write both halves

One oxidation ↑, one reduction ↓.

② Match the electrons

Multiply one or both so they have the same number of electrons.

③ Add them

Reactants on the left, products on the right.

④ Cancel

The electrons (always), and any H+ or H2O on both sides.

⑤ Check

Atoms balance and the total charge is the same on both sides.

Step through a worked example
Multiply the whole half equation, not just the species with the electrons. If you × 5 the Fe2+ half, it's 5Fe2+ → 5Fe3+ + 5e−.
Section 3

Balance the electrons yourself

Change the multipliers until the electrons lost equal the electrons gained. Aim for the lowest common multiple. Then add and cancel.

Section 4

Cancelling H+ and H2O

When both halves contain H+ or H2O, they can end up on both sides after adding. Take the smaller number away from both sides. Sometimes every number then divides by the same amount.

Step through a worked example
Never leave the same species on both sides. 8H+ on the left and 6H+ on the right becomes 2H+ on the left.
Section 5

Oxidising and reducing agents

Oxidising agent

Takes electrons from something else, so it oxidises it. It gains electrons, so it is itself reduced ↓. An electron acceptor.

Reducing agent

Gives electrons to something else, so it reduces it. It loses electrons, so it is itself oxidised ↑. An electron donor.

Which is which?
The agent is named after what it does to the other substance. Oxidation is loss of electrons, so an oxidising agent takes electrons from the other substance. So an oxidising agent gains electrons: it is reduced.

Section 6

Extracting half equations

This is combining in reverse. Oxidation states tell you which species belong in each half.

① Oxidation states

For the atoms in every species.

② Up and down

The atom going ↑ is the oxidation half (e− on the right). The atom going ↓ is the reduction half (e− on the left).

③ Leave out spectators

Ions whose oxidation states don't change, e.g. SO42−, or Cl− in HCl.

④ Balance each half

H+, then H2O, then e−. Divide out the multipliers.

Work through a reaction
The big numbers in front (like the 6 in 6Fe2+) were only there to balance electrons. Divide them out to get the simplest half equation: Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e−.
