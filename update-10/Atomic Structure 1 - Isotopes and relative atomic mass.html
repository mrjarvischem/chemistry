Atomic Structure 1 – Isotopes and Relative Atomic Mass

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.1.1 Atomic structure walkthrough

1. What is an isotope?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons.

So isotopes have the same atomic number (Z) but a different mass number (A).

AZX    mass number A = protons + neutrons  ·  atomic number Z = protons

Compare isotopes

Pick an element. The two (or three) isotopes are drawn side by side. Look at what changes and what stays the same.

● red = proton   ● grey = neutron   ● blue = electron

Why do isotopes have the same chemical properties?

Chemical reactions involve electrons, not the nucleus.

Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons, so a neutral atom has the same number of electrons and the same electron configuration. That means they react in the same way.

The extra neutrons only change the mass. So physical properties that depend on mass (such as density, and how fast a gas diffuses) are slightly different.

Mark scheme warning: "same number of electrons" or "same electron configuration" gets the mark. Just saying "same number of protons" does not, because protons do not take part in reactions.

Key definitions

Relative isotopic mass – the mass of an atom of one isotope compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.

Relative atomic mass, Ar – the average (weighted mean) mass of an atom of an element compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.

Ar = average mass of one atom of the element1/12 × mass of one atom of 12C

The AQA mark scheme gives 1 mark for the top line (average/mean mass of an atom) and 1 mark for the bottom line (1/12 the mass of one atom of 12C). Don't mix "atoms" and "moles" in the same definition.

2. Calculating Ar from isotope data

Ar is a weighted mean. Each isotope's mass is multiplied by how much of it there is, then you divide by the total amount.

Ar = Σ (isotopic mass × abundance)Σ abundance

If the abundances are percentages, the total is 100, so you divide by 100.

If you are given relative abundance (or relative intensity, or peak heights), the numbers don't add up to 100. You must divide by their total.

The see-saw model

Think of each isotope as a pile of blocks sitting on a beam at its mass. The pivot that balances the beam is the Ar. Change the abundances and watch the balance point move towards the most abundant isotope.

3. One missing value

Sometimes one piece of data is missing. There are two common types:

  1. A missing percentage. The percentages must add up to 100, so the missing one is 100 − (the rest). Then calculate Ar as normal.
  2. A missing mass (or abundance) when Ar is given. Write out the Ar equation with the unknown in it and rearrange.

Type 1 – missing percentage, then Ar

4. Two missing abundances – the x method

When two abundances are unknown, you need one letter for both:

  • Call one of them x.
  • The other one is (what's left − x). "What's left" is 100 minus any percentages you already know.
  • Put both into the Ar equation, expand the brackets and solve for x.
Ar × 100 = (known mass × known %) + (mass1 × x) + mass2(remaining − x)

Warm-up – only two isotopes

AQA A-level Paper 1, June 2025, Q07.8

A sample of vanadium contains only 49V and 51V. Ar = 50.97. Calculate the percentage abundance of 51V.

Let 49V = x, so 51V = (100 − x)With only two isotopes, "what's left" is the full 100.
49x + 51(100 − x) = 50.97 × 100 = 5097
49x + 5100 − 51x = 5097Expand the bracket: 51 × 100 and 51 × −x.
5100 − 2x = 5097  →  2x = 3  →  x = 1.5
49V = 1.5%  so  51V = 100 − 1.5 = 98.5%Read the question: it asked for ⁵¹V, not x.

Three isotopes – one percentage given

Magnesium has three isotopes: 24Mg, 25Mg and 26Mg. A sample has Ar = 24.32 and contains 79.0% 24Mg. Find the other two abundances.

Step through the algebra. The bar shows the 100% being shared out.

What's left = 100 − 79.0 = 21.0%This 21.0% is shared between ²⁵Mg and ²⁶Mg.
Let 25Mg = x  and  26Mg = (21.0 − x)
(24 × 79.0) + 25x + 26(21.0 − x) = 24.32 × 100The Ar equation, already multiplied by 100.
1896 + 25x + 546 − 26x = 2432Expand the bracket: 26 × 21.0 = 546 and 26 × −x = −26x.
2442 − x = 2432Collect the numbers (1896 + 546) and the x terms (25x − 26x = −x).
x = 2442 − 2432 = 10.0
25Mg = 10.0%  ·  26Mg = 21.0 − 10.0 = 11.0%Check: 79.0 + 10.0 + 11.0 = 100 ✓
Watch the minus sign. 26(21.0 − x) = 546 − 26x. Forgetting to multiply the x by 26, or losing the minus sign, is where most marks go.

5. Ratio questions

Instead of a percentage, the question tells you the ratio of two (or more) isotopes. Turn the ratio into letters:

  • Ratio 1 : 1 → x and x
  • Ratio 1 : 2 → x and 2x
  • Ratio 2 : 2 : 1 → 2x, 2x and x

Anything left over is 100 − (all those x terms). Then use the same Ar equation as on page 4.

If you're told one percentage and a ratio, you don't need algebra at all: just share out what's left.

Type 1 – percentage given, plus a ratio

AQA A-level Paper 1, June 2023, Q09.4

A sample of strontium contains 86Sr, 87Sr and 88Sr. 82% of the sample is 88Sr. The other isotopes are in a 1 : 2 ratio of 86Sr : 87Sr.

Calculate the percentage abundance of 87Sr. Use it to find Ar to 1 decimal place. [3 marks]

What's left = 100 − 82 = 18%
1 : 2 ratio → 3 parts  →  1 part = 18 ÷ 3 = 6%Add the ratio numbers to get the number of parts.
86Sr = 6%  ·  87Sr = 2 × 6 = 12%
Ar = (86 × 6) + (87 × 12) + (88 × 82)100 = 516 + 1044 + 7216100 = 87.76 = 87.8

A sample of strontium has Ar = 87.7 and contains 86Sr, 87Sr and 88Sr. The ratio 86Sr : 87Sr is 1 : 1. Calculate the percentage abundance of 88Sr.
