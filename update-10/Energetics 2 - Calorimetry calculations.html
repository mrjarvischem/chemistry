Calorimetry calculations

Using q = mcΔT to turn a temperature change into an enthalpy change, for burning fuels and for reactions in solution.

Measuring heat energy: q = mcΔT

We cannot measure enthalpy directly. Instead we let the reaction heat (or cool) some water and measure the temperature change of the water. This is calorimetry.

q = m × c × ΔT

qheat energy transferredJDivide by 1000 to get kJ before finding ΔH.
mmass of the water or solution being heatedgNever the mass of the fuel or solid. 1 cm³ of water or dilute solution = 1 g.
cspecific heat capacity of waterJ g⁻¹ K⁻¹4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. It is given in the question.

CHECK 1

150 g of water is heated from 21.0 °C to 31.0 °C. Calculate q.

ΔT = 10.0 K   q = 150 × 4.18 × 10.0 = 6270 J (6.27 kJ)

CHECK 2

2.18 kJ of heat energy is transferred to 100 g of water. Calculate the temperature rise.

Rearrange: ΔT = q ÷ (m × c) = 2180 ÷ (100 × 4.18) = 5.2 °C. Remember to put q back into J first.

From q to ΔH

The four steps

  1. ΔT = final − initial temperature
  2. q = mcΔT, then ÷ 1000 to get kJ
  3. n = moles of the fuel burned (or of the limiting reagent)
  4. ΔH = −q ÷ n   (kJ mol⁻¹)

Where does the minus sign come from?

q is the energy gained by the water (the surroundings). The reaction (the system) lost exactly that much.

  • Temperature rose → exothermic → ΔH is negative
  • Temperature fell → endothermic → ΔH is positive

Run the same fuel with "No heat loss" and then "Real lab". Which gives the less exothermic value, and why?

The real lab. Some of the heat from the flame goes into the air and the copper can instead of the water, so ΔT is smaller, q is smaller and ΔH is less exothermic.

Combustion calorimetry

Worked example

A student burns 0.0300 mol of an alcohol. It heats 0.250 kg of water in a copper calorimeter from 18.9 °C to 78.1 °C. Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of the alcohol. (c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)

1
Temperature change: ΔT = 78.1 − 18.9 = 59.2 K
2
Mass of water in grams: 0.250 kg = 250 g
3
q = mcΔT = 250 × 4.18 × 59.2 = 61 864 J = 61.864 kJ
4
ΔH = −q ÷ n = −61.864 ÷ 0.0300 = −2062
5
The temperature rose, so ΔH is negative. To 3 s.f.:

ΔcH = −2060 kJ mol⁻¹

The data book value for propan-1-ol is −2021 kJ mol⁻¹, so that is the most likely alcohol.

Spot the error

Each student has made one mistake. Find it.

q = 0.46 × 4.18 × 16.4

0.46 g is the mass of fuel. m is the mass of water heated.

ΔT = 35.1 + 273 = 308.1 K

ΔT is a change: final − initial. Never add 273 to a temperature change.

ΔH = −61 864 ÷ 0.0300 kJ mol⁻¹

q is still in J. Divide by 1000 first to get kJ mol⁻¹.

Water heated from 19 °C to 45 °C: ΔH = +2060 kJ mol⁻¹

The temperature rose, so the reaction is exothermic and ΔH is negative.

0.920 g of ethanol (Mr = 46.0) is burned and heats 200 g of water by 21.5 °C. Calculate ΔcH.

q = 200 × 4.18 × 21.5 = 17 974 J = 17.974 kJ
n = 0.920 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0200 mol
ΔcH = −17.974 ÷ 0.0200 = −899 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 5

0.600 g of butan-1-ol (Mr = 74.0) heats 150 g of water from 19.2 °C to 44.6 °C. Calculate ΔcH to 3 significant figures.

ΔT = 25.4 K   q = 150 × 4.18 × 25.4 = 15 926 J = 15.926 kJ
n = 0.600 ÷ 74.0 = 8.108 × 10⁻³ mol
ΔcH = −15.926 ÷ 8.108 × 10⁻³ = −1960 kJ mol⁻¹

Reactions in solution

For neutralisation, displacement and dissolving, the reaction happens in the water. The solution itself is the surroundings, and a polystyrene cup with a lid keeps the heat in.

m

The total volume of solution, taken as 1.00 g per cm³.

25.0 cm³ + 25.0 cm³ → m = 50.0 g. Do not add the mass of a solid.

n

Moles of the limiting reagent: n = c × V ÷ 1000.

If one reagent is "in excess", use the other one.

ΔT

Highest (or lowest) temperature reached − starting temperature.

Walkthrough 3 shows a more accurate way using a graph.

Worked example: neutralisation

25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl reacts with 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises by 6.8 °C.

1
Mass of solution: 25.0 + 25.0 = 50.0 cm³ → 50.0 g
2
q = mcΔT = 50.0 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 1421 J = 1.421 kJ
3
n(HCl) = 1.00 × 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 mol
4
ΔH = −q ÷ n = −1.421 ÷ 0.0250. The temperature rose, so ΔH is negative.

ΔH = −56.8 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 6

Excess zinc powder is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄(aq). The temperature rises by 25.5 °C. Calculate ΔH for the reaction.

q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 25.5 = 5330 J = 5.330 kJ
n(CuSO₄) = 0.500 × 50.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 mol (zinc is in excess)
ΔH = −5.330 ÷ 0.0250 = −213 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 7

50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises by 6.6 °C. Calculate ΔH per mole of water formed.

m = 100 g   q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.6 = 2759 J = 2.759 kJ
HCl is limiting: n = 1.00 × 50.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0500 mol (NaOH is 0.0600 mol, in excess)
ΔH = −2.759 ÷ 0.0500 = −55.2 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 8

8.00 g of ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (Mr = 80.0), dissolves in 100 g of water. The temperature falls from 20.5 °C to 14.2 °C. Calculate ΔH.

ΔT = 6.3 K   q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.3 = 2633 J = 2.633 kJ
n = 8.00 ÷ 80.0 = 0.100 mol
The temperature fell, so ΔH is positive: ΔH = +26.3 kJ mol⁻¹

Working backwards: finding a temperature

Some questions give you ΔH and ask for the temperature change or the final temperature. Run the same steps in reverse.

Worked example

50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, both at 18.5 °C. ΔH = −57.1 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the final temperature.

1
n(HCl) = 0.500 × 50.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 mol
2
q = n × ΔH = 0.0250 × 57.1 = 1.4275 kJ = 1427.5 J
3
Rearrange q = mcΔT. The mass is the total volume, 100 g.
ΔT = q ÷ (m × c) = 1427.5 ÷ (100 × 4.18) = 3.4 °C
4
ΔH is negative, so the temperature rises: 18.5 + 3.4

Final temperature = 21.9 °C

CHECK 9

0.460 g of ethanol (Mr = 46.0) is burned under 150 g of water at 20.0 °C. ΔcH = −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. Assuming no heat loss, calculate the final temperature of the water.

n = 0.460 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0100 mol   q = 0.0100 × 1367 = 13.67 kJ = 13 670 J
ΔT = 13 670 ÷ (150 × 4.18) = 21.8 °C
Final temperature = 20.0 + 21.8 = 41.8 °C

CHECK 10

25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, both at 19.0 °C. ΔH = −57.1 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the final temperature.

n = 2.00 × 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0500 mol   q = 0.0500 × 57.1 = 2.855 kJ = 2855 J
ΔT = 2855 ÷ (50.0 × 4.18) = 13.7 °C
Final temperature = 19.0 + 13.7 = 32.7 °C

CHECK 11

In a real experiment the final temperature in CHECK 9 would be lower than your answer. Explain why.

Some heat is lost to the air and to the calorimeter, and some fuel burns incompletely, so less energy reaches the water.
