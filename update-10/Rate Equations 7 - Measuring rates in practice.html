1. Two ways to measure rate

Continuous monitoring

Follow one reaction mixture over time, measuring something that changes as it reacts.

→ concentration–time graph → tangents give rates, half-lives show the order.

Initial rates (often a clock reaction)

Do a series of separate experiments with different starting concentrations, and measure only the start of each.

→ initial rate (or 1/t) for each → rates table → orders.

2. Continuous monitoring: collecting a gas

Animation: gas syringe

Each point is recorded when the plunger passes a 10 cm³ mark.

  • Start the stopwatch as soon as the reactants are mixed. Put the bung in quickly so no gas escapes.
  • Initial rate = gradient of the tangent at t = 0 on the volume–time graph. Units: cm³ s⁻¹.
  • An upturned burette or measuring cylinder over water also works, but only for gases that are not very soluble.
QUICK CHECK 2 Measuring rates of reaction Q8

Why is it more accurate to record the time at each 10 cm³ mark than the volume every 10 seconds?

The plunger is moving, so it's hard to read a scale at an exact moment. It's easier to stop the stopwatch (or press "lap") as the plunger passes a fixed mark. Reading a stopwatch is precise, while reading a moving scale isn't.

Colorimetry

  • Use a filter of the complementary colour, e.g. a blue filter for a yellow-brown iodine solution, so the coloured species absorbs strongly.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank (solvent or water).
  • Make a calibration curve of absorbance against known concentrations, then read off concentrations during the reaction.
  • Advantage: it doesn't disturb the mixture and can take readings continuously.

3. Continuous monitoring: quench and titrate

  1. Start the reaction and the timer.
  2. At set times, remove a sample with a pipette.
  3. Quench it: stop the reaction so the concentration is "frozen" at that moment. You can't titrate in seconds!
  4. Titrate each sample to find the concentration of a reactant or product.
  5. Plot concentration (or titre) against time.

Quenching methods: dilute in a large volume of cold water, cool in an ice bath, or add a chemical that removes a reactant or catalyst. The chemical must not interfere with the titration.

QUICK CHECK 4 – making other reactants "disappear" June 2018 Paper 3 Q1.1 (2 marks)

Explain why using a large excess of H₂O₂ and I⁻ means the rate depends only on [H⁺].

M1 the concentrations of H₂O₂ and I⁻ change by a negligible amount (they stay effectively constant)
M2 so they have a constant effect on the rate (they act as if zero order). Only [H⁺] changes.

The 6-mark method question

June 2018 Paper 3 Q1.8: A + B + C → D + E, where E is dark blue and a small amount of X removes E as it forms. Describe experiments to find the order with respect to A.

Tick what your answer includes, then see what level it would reach.

Measure set volumes of B, C and a small fixed amount of X into a flask, and measure A separately. Add A, mix and start the timer immediately. Stop the timer when the blue colour appears (X has run out). Keep the volumes of B, C and X, the total volume (make up with water) and the temperature (water bath) the same. Repeat with different volumes of A. 1/t is proportional to the initial rate. Plot 1/t against [A]: a straight line through the origin means first order, a horizontal line means zero order, and an upward curve means second order. Or compare: doubling [A] doubles 1/t means first order.

5. Control variables and uncertainty

QUICK CHECK 5 June 2025 Paper 3 Q2.1 (3 marks): iodine clock with starch and a fixed amount of thiosulfate

Any 3: temperature; amount/volume of starch; total volume of the reaction mixture; stirring; same shade of colour when the timer is stopped (or the same person judging it).
Ignored: same stopwatch, same apparatus, "same person" with no reason, concentrations of the reactants (those are being varied, or the question already controls them).

Uncertainty in timing

  • A stopwatch reads to ±0.01 s, but the real uncertainty is much bigger: human reaction time and judging when the colour change happens.
  • That is why times are usually recorded to the nearest second. (November 2020 AS Paper 2 Q2.1)
  • Percentage uncertainty = uncertaintymeasured value × 100
  • To reduce it, make the time longer: use more thiosulfate, or a lower temperature.
