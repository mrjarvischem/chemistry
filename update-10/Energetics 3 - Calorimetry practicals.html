Calorimetry of fuels: the practical

The aim is to find an experimental value for the enthalpy of combustion of a liquid fuel, such as an alcohol, by using it to heat a known mass of water.

1
Measure 100 cm³ of water (100 g) into the copper calorimeter and clamp it in place.
2
Stir and record the initial temperature of the water.
3
Weigh the spirit burner with its cap on.
4
Put the burner under the calorimeter, inside a draught shield, and light it. Stir the water all the time.
5
When the temperature has risen by about 20 °C, put the cap back on to put out the flame. Record the highest temperature reached.
6
Reweigh the burner and cap straight away.

Results table

Mass of water / g100m in q = mcΔT
Initial mass of burner / g186.42mass of fuel burned → n = m ÷ Mr

Initial temperature of water / °C19.5ΔT

Units go in the headings, not next to each number. Balance readings are recorded to 2 decimal places, thermometer readings to the precision of the thermometer.

CHECK 1

The fuel in the table above is ethanol (Mr = 46.0). Use the readings to calculate an experimental value for ΔcH.

Mass burned = 186.42 − 185.86 = 0.56 g   n = 0.56 ÷ 46.0 = 0.01217 mol
ΔT = 41.0 − 19.5 = 21.5 K   q = 100 × 4.18 × 21.5 = 8987 J = 8.987 kJ
ΔcH = −8.987 ÷ 0.01217 = −738 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 2

Give two ways of measuring out 100 g of water.

Weigh it directly on a balance, or measure 100 cm³ with a measuring cylinder (density of water = 1.00 g cm⁻³).

Why is the answer so far out?

The data book value for ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. The experiment on the last page gave −738 kJ mol⁻¹, barely half. The experimental value is always less exothermic than the true one. You need to be able to say why, and link it to the calculation.

Source of errorEffect on the calculationImprovement
Heat lost to the air and to the calorimeterΔT too small → q too small → ΔH less exothermicDraught shield, lid on the calorimeter, insulate the sides
Incomplete combustion: soot (C) and CO are madeLess energy released per mole burned → ΔT too smallGood air supply; keep the calorimeter close to, but not smothering, the flame
Fuel evaporates from the hot wickMass "burned" is too big → n too big → ΔH less exothermicCap the burner as soon as it is put out, and weigh straight away
Not standard conditions: water forms as steam, not liquidLess energy released than the standard valueCannot easily be fixed in a school lab
The assumption in every calculation: all the heat energy released by the fuel goes into the water.

CHECK 3

Suggest two disadvantages of using a glass beaker on a tripod and gauze instead of a clamped copper calorimeter.

Glass is a poorer conductor of heat than copper, so less heat reaches the water. The tripod and gauze absorb heat themselves and fix the beaker at one height above the flame.

CHECK 4

Why should the water be stirred throughout?

So the temperature is the same throughout the water and the thermometer reading is representative.

CHECK 5

A student forgets to put the cap back on before reweighing. Explain the effect on the calculated ΔcH.

Fuel evaporates, so the measured mass loss is bigger than the mass actually burned. n is too big, so ΔcH = −q ÷ n is less exothermic.

CHECK 6

The bottom of the calorimeter is black after the experiment. What does this show, and how does it affect the result?

Soot (carbon) has formed, so combustion was incomplete. Less energy is released per mole of fuel, so ΔcH is less exothermic.

Required Practical 2: measuring an enthalpy change in solution

RP2 measures ΔH for a reaction in solution, here zinc displacing copper from copper(II) sulfate:

Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

The problem

The reaction is not instant. While the temperature is still climbing, heat is already leaking out of the cup. The highest thermometer reading is therefore lower than it should be. The fix is to take readings over time and use a graph to find the temperature the mixture would have reached if the reaction had been instant.

Method

1
Use a pipette or burette to put 25.0 cm³ of copper(II) sulfate solution into a polystyrene cup standing in a beaker. Add a lid with a hole for the thermometer.
2
Weigh out an excess of zinc powder (about 3 g).
3
Start a timer. Stir and record the temperature of the solution every minute for 3 minutes.
4
At 4 minutes add the zinc and stir. Do not take a temperature reading at 4 minutes.
5
From 5 minutes, keep stirring and record the temperature every minute for about 10 more minutes.
6
Plot temperature against time and extrapolate to find ΔT at 4 minutes (next page).

CHECK 7

Why is the zinc used as a powder and in excess?

Powder has a large surface area, so the reaction is fast and less heat is lost while it happens. Excess zinc makes sure all the copper(II) sulfate reacts, so the moles of CuSO₄ are the moles that react.

CHECK 8

Why is the temperature recorded for 3 minutes before the zinc is added?

To get a steady, reliable starting temperature. The line through these points is extended forward to the time of mixing.

Finding ΔT from the graph

1
Plot the readings taken before the zinc was added. They give a steady starting temperature of 19.5 °C.
2
Plot the readings taken after the zinc was added at 4 minutes. The temperature climbs, then slowly falls as heat is lost.
3
The highest reading is 43.3 °C. Using it gives ΔT = 43.3 − 19.5 = 23.8 °C, but heat was already escaping while the temperature was rising, so this is too small.
4
Draw a line of best fit through the starting temperatures and extend it forward to 4 minutes.
5
Draw a line of best fit through the cooling points only and extrapolate it back to 4 minutes. It reaches 44.0 °C.
6
ΔT is the vertical gap between the two lines at the time of mixing:

ΔT = 44.0 − 19.5 = 24.5 °C

Do not extrapolate further back than the time of mixing. Ignore the points where the temperature is still rising when you draw the cooling line.

The calculation

25.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄ was used with 3.00 g of zinc (Ar = 65.4). ΔT = 24.5 °C.

1
q = mcΔT = 25.0 × 4.18 × 24.5 = 2560 J = 2.560 kJ   (m is the 25.0 g of solution, not the zinc)
2
Find the limiting reagent. n(CuSO₄) = 0.500 × 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0125 mol   n(Zn) = 3.00 ÷ 65.4 = 0.0459 mol
3
The ratio is 1 : 1, so zinc is in excess and CuSO₄ is limiting. Use 0.0125 mol.
4
ΔH = −2.560 ÷ 0.0125. The temperature rose, so ΔH is negative.

ΔH = −205 kJ mol⁻¹

With the highest reading (ΔT = 23.8 °C) the answer would have been −199 kJ mol⁻¹: less exothermic.

The same method for an endothermic change

When ammonium nitrate dissolves, the temperature falls. Now heat leaks in from the room while the solid is still dissolving, so the lowest reading is not low enough. The graph method fixes this in the same way.

1
Starting temperature of the water: steady at 21.0 °C.
2
The solid is added at 3 minutes. The temperature drops, then slowly creeps back up as heat comes in from the room.
3
The lowest reading is 13.6 °C, giving ΔT = 7.4 °C. This is too small.
4
Extend the starting line forward to 3 minutes.
5
Draw the best-fit line through the warming points and extrapolate back to 3 minutes: 13.0 °C.
6

ΔT = 21.0 − 13.0 = 8.0 °C

CHECK 9

In this experiment 5.00 g of NH₄NO₃ (Mr = 80.0) was dissolved in 50.0 g of water. Use ΔT = 8.0 °C to calculate the enthalpy change.

q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 8.0 = 1672 J = 1.672 kJ   n = 5.00 ÷ 80.0 = 0.0625 mol
The temperature fell, so ΔH is positive: ΔH = 1.672 ÷ 0.0625 = +26.8 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 10 · 4 marks

Describe the steps you would take to determine an accurate minimum temperature that is not influenced by heat from the surroundings.

  • Record the temperature of the water at regular intervals (e.g. every minute) for a few minutes before adding the solid.
  • Add the solid at a known time, stir, and keep recording the temperature at regular intervals for several minutes after.
  • Plot a graph of temperature against time.
  • Extrapolate both lines to the time of mixing and read off the temperature difference there.

Percentage uncertainty

% uncertainty = (uncertainty in each reading × number of readings) ÷ value measured × 100

A temperature change comes from two readings, and so does a mass found by difference, so the uncertainty counts twice.

Thermometer ±0.1 °C, ΔT = 24.5 °C:   (2 × 0.1) ÷ 24.5 × 100 = 0.82 %
Balance ±0.01 g, mass of fuel burned = 0.56 g:   (2 × 0.01) ÷ 0.56 × 100 = 3.6 %

To reduce the percentage uncertainty without changing the apparatus, make the measured value bigger: burn more fuel, or use a more concentrated solution so ΔT is larger.

CHECK 11

A thermometer has an uncertainty of ±0.1 °C. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in a temperature change of 8.0 °C.

(2 × 0.1) ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 2.5 %
