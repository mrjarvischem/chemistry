Rate Equations 6 – The Arrhenius Equation: Graphs

AQA A-level Chemistry · Kinetics walkthrough

1. Turning a curve into a straight line

A graph of k against T is a steep curve, and you can't get Ea from a curve easily. Taking logs turns the Arrhenius equation into the equation of a straight line:

ln k = −EaR × 1T + ln A
 y  =   m   ×  x   +  c
  • Plot ln k on the y-axis and 1/T on the x-axis.
  • Gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R. Divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹.
  • y-intercept = ln A. It's at 1/T = 0, usually far off the graph.
  • The gradient is negative: as 1/T increases (colder), ln k decreases.

Steeper line = bigger Ea = rate more sensitive to temperature.

2. Completing the table

  • Convert to kelvin first: T/K = T/°C + 273.
  • 1/T values are small (about 0.003). Write them to 3 s.f., e.g. 3.41 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹.
  • ln k uses the ln button, not log. ln k is negative whenever k < 1.
  • Give ln k to 3 s.f. AQA has refused 3.29 when the answer was 3.297.

3. From gradient to activation energy

  1. Plot the points. Choose a y-scale so they fill more than half the grid. The y-axis will be negative.
  2. Draw a straight line of best fit, with points evenly either side. Ignore any anomaly.
  3. Draw a big triangle using two points on your line, not data points.
  4. Gradient = Δ(ln k) ÷ Δ(1/T). It will be a large negative number, e.g. −13 000.
  5. Ea = −gradient × 8.31 (J mol⁻¹), then ÷ 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹. Ea must be positive.

4. Clock reactions (1/t) and finding A

If you only have times from a clock reaction, you can still find Ea. Rate ∝ 1/t, so k ∝ 1/t (all the concentrations are the same), and

ln(1/t) = −EaR × 1T + constant

The gradient is the same as for ln k, so Ea comes out the same way. Only the intercept changes.

Finding A from the graph

The intercept (ln A) is usually far off the graph. Instead, pick any point on your line and substitute it into the equation:

ln A = ln k + EaR × 1T   then   A = eln A
