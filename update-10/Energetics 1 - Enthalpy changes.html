Enthalpy changes

What ΔH means, standard conditions, and the three enthalpy changes you must be able to define and write equations for.

What is an enthalpy change?

Enthalpy change, ΔH

The heat energy change measured at constant pressure.

Units: kJ mol⁻¹. Δ means "change in"; H is enthalpy, the energy stored in the chemicals.

Standard conditions

To compare values fairly, enthalpy changes are quoted under the same conditions. The symbol is ΔH.

Pressure100 kPa
Temperature298 K (25 °C)
Solutions1 mol dm⁻³

Standard states

The standard state of a substance is its physical state at 298 K and 100 kPa.

H₂O(l)   C(s)   O₂(g)   Br₂(l)   Na(s)

Carbon's standard state is solid graphite. Bromine and mercury are the two elements that are liquids.

Why "at constant pressure"?

Reactions in beakers and flasks are open to the air, so they stay at atmospheric pressure. If a gas is made, the mixture can expand instead of the pressure building up. The heat we measure under those conditions is ΔH.

CHECK 1

State the conditions used for standard enthalpy changes.

100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K. Every substance is in its standard state (and solutions are 1 mol dm⁻³).

CHECK 2

Give the standard state of each: water, ethanol, carbon dioxide, magnesium, bromine.

H₂O(l), C₂H₅OH(l), CO₂(g), Mg(s), Br₂(l)

Exothermic and endothermic: the sign of ΔH

The reacting chemicals are the system. Everything else (the water, the beaker, the air, the thermometer) is the surroundings. Pick a reaction type and watch where the energy goes.

The thermometer measures the surroundings, not the system. When the temperature goes up, the system has lost energy, so ΔH is negative. This is why ΔH and ΔT always have opposite signs.

CHECK 3

A reaction has ΔH = +52 kJ mol⁻¹. Is it exothermic or endothermic, and what happens to the temperature of the surroundings?

Endothermic. Heat is taken in from the surroundings, so their temperature falls.

CHECK 4

Ammonium nitrate dissolves in water and the beaker feels cold. What is the sign of ΔH? Explain.

Positive. The water (surroundings) has lost heat energy to the system, so the system's enthalpy has gone up.

Enthalpy change of combustion, ΔcH

Standard enthalpy of combustion

The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states.

1
One mole of methane meets oxygen. The fuel is the substance being burned, so it must have a 1 in front of it.
2
It burns completely: all the carbon ends up as CO₂ (not CO or soot) and all the hydrogen as H₂O. Heat is given out.
3
Write it with state symbols. Water is H₂O(l) because that is its standard state at 298 K.

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

4
ΔcH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹. Combustion is always exothermic, so ΔcH is always negative.

Worked example: write the ΔcH equation for butane, C₄H₁₀

1
Start with 1 mole of fuel in its standard state:   C₄H₁₀(g) + …O₂(g) →
2
Complete combustion makes CO₂(g) and H₂O(l).
3
Balance C: 4 carbon atoms → 4CO₂
4
Balance H: 10 hydrogen atoms → 5H₂O
5
Balance O last: (4 × 2) + 5 = 13 oxygen atoms → 6½O₂. Fractions are fine; the fuel must stay at 1 mol.
6

C₄H₁₀(g) + 6½O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l)    ΔcH = −2877 kJ mol⁻¹

Spot the error

None of these is a correct standard enthalpy of combustion equation. Find the mistake before you reveal it.

2C₂H₆(g) + 7O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l)

2 moles of fuel. Halve it: C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)

Water must be H₂O(l), its standard state at 298 K.

CH₄(g) + 1½O₂(g) → CO(g) + 2H₂O(l)

Incomplete combustion. The carbon must end up as CO₂.

C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

Not balanced. 8 hydrogen atoms need 4H₂O(l).

CHECK 5

Write an equation for the standard enthalpy of combustion of propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH.

C₃H₇OH(l) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)
Count the O already in the alcohol: 6 + 4 = 10 O atoms needed, 1 comes from the alcohol, so 9 from O₂ = 4½O₂.

CHECK 6

Explain why C₂H₅OH(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l) does not represent the standard enthalpy of combustion of ethanol.

Ethanol is a liquid at 298 K, so its standard state is C₂H₅OH(l), not (g).

Enthalpy change of formation, ΔfH

Standard enthalpy of formation

The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements, with all reactants and products in their standard states.

Worked example: write the ΔfH equation for ethanol, C₂H₅OH

1
Write 1 mole of the compound on the right, in its standard state:   → C₂H₅OH(l)
2
List its elements in their standard states: C(s), H₂(g), O₂(g)
3
Balance C: 2 carbon atoms → 2C(s)
4
Balance H: 6 hydrogen atoms → 3H₂(g)
5
Balance O: 1 oxygen atom → ½O₂(g). Never double the equation to remove the fraction: the product must stay at 1 mol.
6

2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)    ΔfH = −277 kJ mol⁻¹

More examples

EquationΔfH / kJ mol⁻¹Key point
H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)−286½O₂ makes exactly 1 mol of water. This is also ΔcH of hydrogen.
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)−394Also ΔcH of carbon.
Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s)−411Chlorine exists as Cl₂ molecules, not Cl atoms.
Mg(s) + ½O₂(g) → MgO(s)−602Metals are solids in their standard states.

Why is ΔfH of an element zero?

An element in its standard state cannot be formed from itself. Nothing changes, no bonds are broken or made, so ΔfH = 0. This only applies to the standard state. Decide whether each of these has ΔfH = 0, then click to check.

Spot the error

2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)

2 moles are formed. Halve it: Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s)

2H(g) + O(g) → H₂O(l)

Gaseous atoms are not standard states. Use H₂(g) and ½O₂(g).

CaO(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)

Made from compounds, not elements. It should be Ca(s) + C(s) + 1½O₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)

½H₂(g) + ½Br₂(g) → HBr(g)

Bromine is a liquid at 298 K: ½Br₂(l).

Write an equation for the standard enthalpy of formation of nitric acid, HNO₃(l).

CHECK 9

Explain why ΔfH of Na(l) is not zero, and predict its sign.

Sodium's standard state is Na(s). Making Na(l) means melting the solid, which takes in energy, so ΔfH is positive.

Bond dissociation enthalpy and mean bond enthalpy

Bond dissociation enthalpy

The enthalpy change to break 1 mole of a covalent bond, with all species in the gaseous state.

Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g)    ΔH = +242 kJ mol⁻¹

  • Always positive. Breaking a bond takes energy in (endothermic).
  • Making the same bond gives the same amount out: −242 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Everything is a gas, and the products are separate atoms.

Mean bond enthalpy

1
Breaking all four C–H bonds in methane:   CH₄(g) → C(g) + 4H(g)   ΔH = +1664 kJ mol⁻¹
2
Four bonds are broken, so the average for one C–H bond in methane is 1664 ÷ 4 = +416 kJ mol⁻¹
3
The data book value for C–H is +413 kJ mol⁻¹. It is different because the strength of a C–H bond changes slightly from one molecule to another.
4

Mean bond enthalpy

The bond dissociation enthalpy averaged over a range of different compounds.

This is why calculations using mean bond enthalpies only give approximate answers. More on this in Walkthrough 5.

Spot the error

Br₂(l) → 2Br(g)

Br₂ must be gaseous. As written, the value would include boiling the liquid as well.

Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g)   ΔH = −242

Wrong sign. Breaking bonds is endothermic: +242.

½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g)

Only half a mole of bonds is broken. Start from 1 mol of Cl₂(g).

HCl(g) → H⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g)

The bond breaks to give atoms, not ions: HCl(g) → H(g) + Cl(g)

CHECK 10

For H₂O(g) → 2H(g) + O(g), ΔH = +928 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the mean O–H bond enthalpy in water.

Two O–H bonds are broken: 928 ÷ 2 = +464 kJ mol⁻¹

CHECK 11

For NH₃(g) → N(g) + 3H(g), ΔH = +1173 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the mean N–H bond enthalpy in ammonia.

Three N–H bonds are broken: 1173 ÷ 3 = +391 kJ mol⁻¹
