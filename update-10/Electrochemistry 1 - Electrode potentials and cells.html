Section 1

Electrode potentials

Magnesium is more reactive than copper: it loses electrons more easily. An electrode potential measures that tendency in volts, for any half equation, compared with a standard reference electrode.

Electrode potentials are always quoted for the half equation written as a reduction, with the electrons on the left and a ⇌ sign, e.g. Zn2+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ Zn(s).

More negative E⦵

The equilibrium lies further to the left. The reduced form (on the right) loses electrons more easily: it is more easily oxidised.

More positive E⦵

The equilibrium lies further to the right. The oxidised form (on the left) gains electrons more easily: it is more easily reduced.

Pick any two half equations to compare
Standard electrode potential, E⦵: the electrode potential measured under standard conditions (298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm−3 solutions) against a standard hydrogen electrode, which is given the value 0.00 V. More on that in Part 2.
Section 2

Half cells

A half cell is a conductor (the electrode) dipping into a solution of the ions in the half equation. There are three types.

Metal and its ions

The metal is the electrode, in a 1.00 mol dm−3 solution of its ions. Fe in Fe2+(aq).

Two ions

No solid metal in the half equation, so use a platinum electrode in a mixture of both ions, 1.00 mol dm−3 each.

A gas

Bubble the gas at 100 kPa over a platinum electrode dipping into a 1.00 mol dm−3 solution of the ions.

Choose a half equation to see its half cell
Why platinum? It is a good conductor of electrons and it is inert: it won't react with the solution, so it doesn't change the potential.
Section 3

Changing conditions

A half cell is an equilibrium, so Le Chatelier applies. Change a concentration (or the pressure of a gas) and the equilibrium shifts, which changes the electrode potential. That's why E⦵ values are only quoted under standard conditions.

Change the concentrations
The rule. Anything that shifts the equilibrium to the right (more of the species on the left, or less of the one on the right) makes the electrode potential more positive. A shift to the left makes it more negative.
Section 4

Building an electrochemical cell

Join two half cells and you have an electrochemical cell. Two connections are needed to complete the circuit.

A wire

Carries electrons between the electrodes, from the more negative electrode to the more positive one.

A salt bridge

Lets ions move between the solutions to balance the charge. Usually filter paper soaked in saturated potassium nitrate: soluble and unreactive.

A high-resistance voltmeter

Measures the potential difference, the cell emf, while letting almost no current flow.

Zinc and copper half cells
A wire can't replace the salt bridge: metals carry electrons, but the solutions need ions to move. Chloride salt bridges are avoided with silver or lead half cells, because they would form a precipitate.
Section 5

Calculating the cell emf

Ecell = Eright − Eleft   The more negative electrode goes on the left (it is oxidised). The more positive one goes on the right (it is reduced). Written this way, Ecell is always positive.
Build a cell: choose the left and right half cells
Never multiply an E⦵ value, even if you double the half equation to balance electrons. It's a property of the half cell, not of how many electrons react.
Section 6

Standard cell representations

A cell can be written in one line instead of drawn. Read it left to right, the way the electrons go.

Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)   Ecell = +1.10 V

Left = oxidation

The more negative half cell. Write it in the direction it reacts: reduced form | oxidised form.

Right = reduction

The more positive half cell: oxidised form | reduced form.

The lines

| is a phase boundary (solid | solution). || is the salt bridge. A comma separates two species in the same phase.

Platinum

Goes on the outside at either end: Pt(s) | Fe2+(aq), Fe3+(aq) on the left; Fe3+(aq), Fe2+(aq) | Pt(s) on the right.

Build the cell representation: click the pieces in order
The electrodes are always at the far ends. Electrons leave from the left-hand electrode and arrive at the right-hand one.
