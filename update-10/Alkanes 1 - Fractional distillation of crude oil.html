Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.3.2 Alkanes · walkthrough 1

1. Crude oil and alkanes

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons – mostly alkanes (plus some cycloalkanes and aromatics).

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons: only C–C single bonds, and only C and H. General formula CnH2n+2.

The mixture is no use as it is. It is separated by fractional distillation into fractions.

A fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points (so a similar number of carbon atoms).

A fraction is not a single compound – "kerosene" is a mixture. And fractional distillation is a physical process: no covalent bonds are broken, only the forces between molecules are overcome.

2. How the fractionating column works

  1. Crude oil is heated in a furnace (about 400 °C) so most of it vaporises.
  2. The vapour goes into the bottom of the column. There is a temperature gradient: hot at the bottom, cooler at the top.
  3. Vapours rise up the column, passing through bubble caps in the trays, and cool down.
  4. Each compound condenses when it reaches a tray where the temperature is below its boiling point, and is piped off.
  5. Small molecules (low b.p.) reach the top as gases. Large molecules (high b.p.) condense low down. The biggest never vaporise – the residue leaves at the bottom as a liquid.

3. Boiling point and chain length

Alkanes are non-polar, so the only forces between molecules are van der Waals forces (induced dipole–dipole).

Longer chain → more electrons and more surface contact between molecules → stronger van der Waals forces → more energy needed to separate them → higher boiling point.

Branched isomers have lower boiling points: they can't pack as closely, so there's less surface contact and weaker van der Waals forces.

4. The fractions and their uses

Carbon ranges are approximate – different books give slightly different values. You don't need to learn exact ranges, but you do need the order and the trends.

Going down the column, the fractions have:

  • longer chains and higher boiling points
  • higher viscosity (thicker, flow less easily)
  • lower volatility (evaporate less easily)
  • lower flammability (harder to ignite)
  • darker colour
