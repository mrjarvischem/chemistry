Kinetics 4 – The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

AQA A-level Chemistry · Kinetics walkthrough

1. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

In a gas, the molecules don't all have the same energy. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shows how many molecules have each energy at a given temperature.

  • y-axis: number (or fraction) of molecules. x-axis: (kinetic) energy.
  • It starts at the origin: no molecules have zero energy.
  • The peak is the most probable energy.
  • The mean energy is a little to the right of the peak, because the curve isn't symmetrical.
  • The curve never touches the x-axis on the right: there's no maximum energy.
  • The area under the curve = the total number of molecules.
  • Only molecules to the right of Ea have enough energy to react.

2. Manipulate the curve: temperature and Ea

T₁T₂ (if shown)molecules with E ≥ Eₐ

At a higher temperature the curve…

  1. has its peak moved to the right (higher most probable energy) and lower;
  2. has the same area (same number of molecules), so it's flatter and more spread out;
  3. crosses the original curve only once;
  4. has a much bigger area beyond Ea. That's why rate rises so steeply with temperature.

3. Changing the number of molecules, and adding a catalyst

Same temperature, different number of molecules

Same temperature → same shape and same peak energy. Fewer molecules → every point is scaled down, so the area is smaller.

Adding a catalyst

The curve doesn't change (same temperature). Ea moves to the left, so a bigger proportion of molecules have E ≥ Ea.

4. The maths: how many molecules have E ≥ Ea?

The fraction of molecules with energy of at least Ea is approximately:

fraction ≈ e−Ea / RT    (Ea in J mol⁻¹, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T in K)

This is an extension. You won't be asked to use it at AS, but it explains why the rate is so sensitive to temperature. You'll meet it again in Year 2 as part of the Arrhenius equation.

Calculator

What the numbers show

  • Only a tiny fraction of molecules can react. At 298 K with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹ it's about 2 in a billion.
  • A 10 K rise increases the mean energy by only about 3%, and the collision frequency by under 2%. But the fraction with E ≥ Ea roughly doubles.
  • That's why exam answers say "many more molecules have E ≥ Ea", not just "more collisions".
