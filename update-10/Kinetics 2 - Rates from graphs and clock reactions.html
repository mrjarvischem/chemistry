1. Rate at a particular moment: tangents

  • The gradient of a graph of amount against time is the rate.
  • A curve has a different gradient at every point. To find the rate at one moment, draw a tangent (a straight line that just touches the curve there) and find its gradient.
  • The tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate.
  • For a reactant the gradient is negative. Quote the rate as a positive number.
gradient = change in y (Δy)change in x (Δx)

Interactive: slide the tangent along the curve

Notice the tangent gets flatter with time: the rate falls because the reactants are being used up.

Drawing a good tangent

  1. Use a ruler. The line should touch the curve at one point and not cut across it.
  2. Make it long. Use a big gradient triangle, at least half the grid, to cut the reading error.
  3. Read two points on the tangent (not the curve) and calculate Δy ÷ Δx.
  4. Units: (y units) ÷ (x units), e.g. cm³ s⁻¹, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, g min⁻¹.

3. Clock reactions: why rate ∝ 1/t

In a clock reaction you time how long it takes to make a fixed amount of product. That's enough to hide a cross (sulfur), or for a colour to appear or disappear.

mean rate = fixed amountt   ⇒   rate ∝ 1t
  • The amount is the same every time, so it's just a constant. A shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Only a small fraction of the reactants has reacted when the clock stops. The curve is still nearly straight, so this mean rate is a good approximation to the initial rate.
  • 1/t values are small, so many questions use 1000/t to get easier numbers. It's still proportional to rate.
