Halogenoalkanes – Elimination

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.3.3 Halogenoalkanes · walkthrough 3

1. Elimination to make alkenes

Heat a halogenoalkane with KOH (or NaOH) dissolved in ethanol, under reflux, and you get an alkene:

CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH → CH₂=CHCH₃ + KBr + H₂O

Here OH⁻ acts as a base – it removes an H⁺ from the carbon next to the C–X carbon. H and X are eliminated and a C=C forms between those two carbons.

Three arrows, in this order: (1) lone pair on O of OH⁻ → the H on the adjacent carbon; (2) that C–H bond → the C–C bond (making C=C); (3) the C–X bond → X. Don't attack the carbon – that's substitution.

3. How many alkenes can form?

The H can be removed from any carbon next to the C–X carbon (including a branch carbon). Different neighbours can give different alkenes – and some of these have E and Z isomers too.

EXAM 3AQA AS Paper 2, June 2025, Q01.4

Under different conditions, 2-bromobutane reacts with KOH to form but-1-ene and two other alkenes. Name and complete the mechanism to form but-1-ene. Identify the other two alkenes. [5 marks]

Mechanism: (base) elimination · arrow from lone pair on O of OH⁻ to an H on C1 · arrow from that C–H bond to the C1–C2 bond · arrow from C–Br to Br.
Other alkenes: E-but-2-ene and Z-but-2-ene.
EXAM 4AQA A-level Paper 3, June 2022, Q03.3

2-Bromobutane reacts with NaOH to give alkenes A, B and C. A is a structural isomer of B and C; A does not show stereoisomerism; B and C are stereoisomers. Deduce the name of A and explain why A does not show stereoisomerism. [2 marks]

but-1-ene – C1 of the C=C has two identical groups (two H atoms), so E/Z isomers can't exist.
