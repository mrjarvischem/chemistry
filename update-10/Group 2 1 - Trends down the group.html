Group 2: trends down the group

Work through the trends in atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point from magnesium to barium, and how to explain each one. Then add sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid to Group 2 solutions to see the two solubility trends, which go in opposite directions.

Section 1

Start with the data

Group 2 elements are in the s block. Each one has two electrons in its outer s sub-shell, which it loses to form a 2+ ion.

Mg [Ne] 3s² · Ca [Ar] 4s² · Sr [Kr] 5s² · Ba [Xe] 6s²

Section 2

Atomic radius increases down the group

Click through the elements. Each step down the group adds a whole new shell (energy level) of electrons, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus.

Choose an element
Full-mark answer: atomic radius increases down the group because each element has one more shell of electrons, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus.
"More electrons" on its own doesn't get the mark. Say more shells (or more energy levels).
Section 3

First ionisation energy decreases down the group

First ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

Ca(g) → Ca+(g) + e−  state symbols (g) are needed
Choose an element, then remove its outer electron

Nuclear charge increases down the group, which on its own would make the electron harder to remove. But two effects outweigh it: the outer electron is further from the nucleus and there is more shielding from the extra inner shells.

Full-mark answer: going down the group the outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus and is more shielded by inner electrons. This outweighs the increase in nuclear charge, so the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker and less energy is needed to remove it.

This is also why reactivity increases down the group: Group 2 metals react by losing their two outer electrons, and that gets easier. You'll see this with water in run-through 2.

Section 4

Melting point: metallic bonding gets weaker

Group 2 metals have metallic bonding: a giant lattice of 2+ ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. To melt the metal you need to overcome the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

Choose a metal
Full-mark answer: going down the group the metal ions get bigger (the charge stays 2+). The delocalised electrons are further from the nucleus of each ion, so the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons gets weaker. Less energy is needed to overcome it, so melting point generally decreases.
Magnesium doesn't fit the trend: its melting point is lower than calcium's because it has a different crystal structure. Don't mention intermolecular forces or ionic bonds anywhere in this answer: it's metallic bonding.
Section 5

Hydroxides: solubility increases down the group

Each tube holds a solution of a Group 2 chloride. Add sodium hydroxide solution. If the Group 2 hydroxide is not very soluble, it comes out as a white precipitate.

Add NaOH(aq) to each solution
Mg2+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s)
The solubility of Group 2 hydroxides increases down the group. Mg(OH)2 is sparingly soluble, so you get the most precipitate. Ba(OH)2 is soluble enough that no precipitate forms. A solution of Ca(OH)2 is limewater.
Section 6

Sulfates: solubility decreases down the group

Same solutions, but this time add dilute sulfuric acid (any solution containing sulfate ions does the same job). Now the precipitate gets heavier down the group.

Add dilute H₂SO₄(aq) to each solution
Ba2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → BaSO4(s)
The solubility of Group 2 sulfates decreases down the group. MgSO4 is soluble; BaSO4 is insoluble. That is the opposite of the hydroxide trend, so learn the two together.
Predict, then click to check

Use the two reagents together: NaOH(aq) picks out Mg²⁺, H₂SO₄(aq) picks out Ba²⁺, and Ca²⁺ and Sr²⁺ are told apart by how much sulfate precipitate forms.
