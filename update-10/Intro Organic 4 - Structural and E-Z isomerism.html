Isomerism

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.3.1 Introduction to organic chemistry · walkthrough 4

1. What are isomers?

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but a different structural formula.

Stereoisomers have the same structural formula but a different arrangement of the atoms in space.

Optical isomerism is the other type of stereoisomerism – you'll meet it in Year 13 (3.3.7).

2. Chain isomers

Chain isomers have the same molecular formula, but a different arrangement of the carbon skeleton – the chain is branched differently.

More branching → molecules are more compact and pack less closely → less surface contact → weaker van der Waals forces → lower boiling point.

Pick a molecular formula. How many isomers can you draw? Then reveal them one at a time.

Bending the chain doesn't make a new isomer. Always find the longest chain and name it – if two drawings have the same name, they're the same compound.

pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane. ("3-methylbutane" is just 2-methylbutane numbered the wrong way.)

3. Position isomers

Position isomers have the same carbon skeleton and the same functional group, but the functional group is in a different position on the chain.

Move the functional group along the chain. Watch for when the name stops changing – that's the same molecule from the other end.

4. Functional group isomers

Functional group isomers have the same molecular formula but different functional groups – they're in different homologous series.

The big one at AS: alkenes and cycloalkanes both have the general formula CnH2n.

To tell an alkene from a cycloalkane, add bromine water: the alkene decolourises it (orange → colourless). The cycloalkane has no C=C, so it stays orange.

EXAM 1AQA AS Paper 2, June 2017, Q03.1

Compounds A, B and C all have the molecular formula C₅H₁₀. A and B decolourise bromine water but C does not. B exists as two stereoisomers but A does not show stereoisomerism. Deduce a possible structure for each of A, B and C and explain your deductions. State the meaning of the term stereoisomers and explain how they arise in compound B. [6 marks]

A and B decolourise bromine water → they contain C=C, so they are alkenes.
C doesn't → no C=C, but still CnH2n, so it's a cycloalkane, e.g. cyclopentane.
B shows stereoisomerism → pent-2-ene. Each C of the C=C has two different groups (H and CH₃ / H and C₂H₅).
A doesn't → e.g. pent-1-ene: one carbon of the C=C has two identical groups (two H).
Stereoisomers: same structural formula but different arrangement of atoms in space.
They arise in B because there is restricted rotation about the C=C, so the groups are fixed on the same side (Z) or opposite sides (E).

5. E/Z stereoisomerism

E/Z isomers happen because there is restricted rotation about the C=C double bond – rotating would break the π bond.

A molecule has E/Z isomers if both:

  1. it has a C=C double bond, and
  2. each carbon of the C=C has two different groups attached.

E/Z or not?

Look at the two groups on each carbon of the C=C. Decide, then check.

6. Deciding E or Z – the CIP priority rules

  1. Look at one carbon of the C=C. Of its two groups, the one whose first atom has the higher atomic number has higher priority. (Br > Cl > O > N > C > H)
  2. If the first atoms are the same, move out to the next atoms and compare those: –CH₂CH₃ beats –CH₃.
  3. Do the same for the other carbon.
  4. Higher-priority groups on the same side → Z (zusammen, together). On opposite sides → E (entgegen, opposite).

Memory aid: Z = "zame zide".

"cis" and "trans" only work when each carbon has an H. E/Z always works – and cis isn't always Z! See example 3.

EXAM 6AQA AS Paper 2, 2020, Q05

Explain the differences between structural isomerism and stereoisomerism. Use examples to show how compounds with the molecular formula C₄H₈ exhibit stereoisomerism and the three types of structural isomerism. [6 marks]

Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural formula.
Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space.
Chain: but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene (different carbon skeleton).
Position: but-1-ene and but-2-ene (C=C in a different place).
Functional group: but-1-ene and cyclobutane (or methylcyclopropane) – alkene vs cycloalkane.
Stereo (E/Z): E-but-2-ene and Z-but-2-ene. Restricted rotation about C=C, and each C has two different groups (H and CH₃).
