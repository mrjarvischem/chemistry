Section 1

The metals with water

Reactivity increases down the group, because the outer electrons are lost more easily. With cold water the products are the hydroxide and hydrogen. Magnesium is the odd one out: it barely reacts with cold water but burns in steam to give the oxide.

Choose a reaction
These are redox reactions. The metal is oxidised (0 → +2) and hydrogen is reduced (+1 → 0). The solutions get more alkaline down the group because the hydroxides get more soluble.
Section 2

Using magnesium to extract titanium

Titanium is strong, light and doesn't corrode, but it is expensive to extract. Step through the process.

Step through the extraction
TiCl4 + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl2  the equation you must know
Magnesium is the reducing agent. Mg is oxidised (0 → +2) and titanium is reduced (+4 → 0). The reaction is done at about 1000 °C in an argon atmosphere, so the hot metals don't react with oxygen or nitrogen in the air.
Section 3

Hydroxides as bases: medicine and farming

Mg(OH)₂ in medicine

"Milk of magnesia" is an antacid: it neutralises excess stomach acid (HCl).

Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O

Ca(OH)₂ in agriculture

Slaked lime is spread on fields to neutralise acidic soil, so crops grow better.

Ca(OH)2 + 2H+ → Ca2+ + 2H2O

Why use sparingly soluble Mg(OH)₂ as an antacid and not NaOH? Add the same amount of base to each beaker of stomach acid and watch the pH.

100 cm³ of 0.10 mol dm⁻³ HCl in each beaker
1 dose neutralises 0.0020 mol H⁺

Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble, so the concentration of OH⁻ ions in solution is always low. Even in excess it can't make the stomach strongly alkaline, so it is safe to swallow. NaOH is soluble and strongly alkaline, so it is corrosive.
Section 4

Removing SO₂ from flue gases

Burning fossil fuels that contain sulfur produces sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain. Power stations remove it by passing the waste gases through a wet slurry of calcium oxide or calcium carbonate. SO₂ is an acidic oxide and CaO is a basic oxide, so they neutralise each other.

Choose the base in the slurry
Bonus: the calcium sulfite can be oxidised by air to calcium sulfate (gypsum), which is sold to make plasterboard: 2CaSO₃ + O₂ → 2CaSO₄
Section 5

Barium sulfate in medicine

Soft tissue like the gut doesn't show up well on an X-ray. A patient drinks a barium meal, a suspension of BaSO₄ in water, which coats the gut so its outline shows on the X-ray.

X-ray

Why barium?

Barium atoms absorb X-rays strongly, so wherever BaSO₄ coats the gut it shows up white.

Why is it safe?

Ba²⁺ ions are toxic, but BaSO₄ is insoluble. It doesn't dissolve, so the barium ions are not absorbed into the blood and it passes through the body.

Exam trap

A soluble barium compound such as BaCl₂ could not be used: it would release toxic Ba²⁺ ions.

Section 6

Testing for sulfate ions, and why we acidify

Barium chloride solution gives a white precipitate of BaSO₄ with sulfate ions. But carbonate ions also give a white precipitate with Ba²⁺ (BaCO₃). Try each solution with and without acid first.

1. Choose a solution · 2. Choose what to acidify with · 3. Add BaCl₂(aq)
Ba2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → BaSO4(s)  white precipitate
2H+(aq) + CO32−(aq) → CO2(g) + H2O(l)  what the acid does to carbonate
Full-mark answer: the solution is acidified (with dilute HCl or HNO₃) to remove carbonate ions, which would otherwise react with Ba²⁺ to form a white precipitate of BaCO₃ and give a false positive. Don't use H₂SO₄, because it adds sulfate ions.
