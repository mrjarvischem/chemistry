1. What is an order of reaction?

The order with respect to a reactant tells you how the rate depends on the concentration of that reactant.

It is the power that the reactant's concentration is raised to in the rate equation.

Order w.r.t. Arate ∝Double [A] → rateTriple [A] → rateIn words
0[A]⁰ = 1× 1 (no change)× 1[A] has no effect on the rate
1[A]¹× 2× 3rate is directly proportional to [A]
2[A]²× 2² = × 4× 3² = × 9rate is proportional to [A] squared

Animation: why would orders be different?

Each box shows a different way that reactant A can react. Change [A] and watch what happens to the rate in each box. Look at what has to happen for one reaction event (✸) in each case.

[A]:

Zero order

A must land on one of the 3 hot surface sites (grey) to react. The sites are always full, so extra A just waits its turn and the rate can't go up.
(e.g. NH₃ decomposing on hot tungsten)

First order

Each A particle has the same small chance per second of breaking apart on its own. Twice as many A particles gives twice as many reactions per second.

Second order

A reacts only when two A particles collide. Double [A]: there are twice as many particles, and each one has twice as many partners to hit. 2 × 2 = 4× the rate.

[A] is held constant in each box (reacted particles are replaced) so we compare the rate at one concentration at a time. The counts are random, so they jump about. Watch the trend.

The big idea

The order tells you how many particles of that reactant are involved in the slowest step of the reaction. You'll meet this again as the rate-determining step.

Orders can only be found by experiment. You cannot read them from the balanced equation.
Example: propanone + I₂ (acid-catalysed) is zero order with respect to I₂, even though I₂ is a reactant in the equation. It is first order with respect to H⁺, even though H⁺ is a catalyst and isn't used up.

2. The rate equation

rate = k [A]m [B]n
  • m and n are the orders with respect to A and B. They are found by experiment and are usually 0, 1 or 2.
  • Overall order = m + n (add up all the orders).
  • k is the rate constant. It is constant at a fixed temperature. Only temperature (or adding a catalyst) changes k. Changing concentration does not change k.
  • A reactant that is zero order is left out of the rate equation, because [X]⁰ = 1.

Calculating k

June 2017 Paper 2 Q3.1 (3 marks)

rate = k[C][D]. At 25 °C the initial rate is 3.1 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ when [C] = 0.48 mol dm⁻³ and [D] = 0.23 mol dm⁻³. Calculate k and give its units.

  1. Rearrange: k = rate[C][D]
  2. k = 3.1 × 10⁻³ ÷ (0.48 × 0.23) = 3.1 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.1104 = 2.8 × 10⁻²
  3. Units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹(mol dm⁻³)(mol dm⁻³) = mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ (more on units on the next page)
QUICK CHECK 3 Rate Equations 2 Q5

The acid-catalysed reaction of propanone with iodine is 1st order with respect to propanone, 1st order with respect to H⁺ and zero order with respect to iodine. [CH₃COCH₃] = 0.10, [I₂] = 0.10 and [H⁺] = 0.50 mol dm⁻³. The rate is 1.52 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Write the rate equation and calculate k.

rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺] (iodine is zero order, so it is left out)
k = 1.52 × 10⁻³ ÷ (0.10 × 0.50) = 3.04 × 10⁻² mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹
Always build the rate equation from the orders, not from the balanced equation.
QUICK CHECK 4 June 2019 Paper 2 Q4.3 (3 marks)

A reaction is second order with respect to R and second order with respect to S. The initial rate is 1.20 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ when [R] = 1.00 × 10⁻² and [S] = 2.45 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³. Calculate k.

rate = k[R]²[S]²
k = 1.20 × 10⁻³ ÷ ((1.00 × 10⁻²)² × (2.45 × 10⁻²)²) = 1.20 × 10⁻³ ÷ 6.00 × 10⁻⁸ = 2.00 × 10⁴ mol⁻³ dm⁹ s⁻¹
Common slip: forgetting to square both concentrations.
QUICK CHECK 5 Rate Equations 2 Q6

Butadiene dimerisation is second order with respect to C₄H₆. When [C₄H₆] = 0.035 mol dm⁻³ the rate is 4.02 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate k.

k = 4.02 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.035² = 4.02 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 1.225 × 10⁻³ = 3.28 × 10⁻³ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹

3. Units of the rate constant, k

The units of k are not fixed. They depend on the overall order. Always work them out by substituting units into the rearranged rate equation and cancelling.

Interactive: units machine

Overall orderUnits of k
0mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
1s⁻¹
2mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹
3mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹
4mol⁻³ dm⁹ s⁻¹

Pattern: each extra order adds another mol⁻¹ dm³. Learn the method, not the table.

Why the units matter

  1. Marks: units of k are almost always a separate mark in the exam.
  2. They tell you the overall order. s⁻¹ means first order overall. mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ means third order overall. So if you're given k with units, you can find a missing order.
  3. A built-in check. If your rate equation gives units that don't match the k you've been given, your rate equation is wrong.

QUICK CHECK 8 Rate Equations 2 Q7

2nd order w.r.t. A, zero order w.r.t. B, 1st order w.r.t. C. Rate = 0.930 mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ when every concentration is 0.100 mol dm⁻³. Calculate k and choose the units.

Units:
rate = k[A]²[C] → 3rd order overall
k = 0.930 ÷ (0.100² × 0.100) = 0.930 ÷ 1.00 × 10⁻³ = 930 mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹
Common errors: getting the signs the wrong way round (mol dm⁻³ when it should be mol⁻¹ dm³); writing dm³ as dm⁻³; leaving out s⁻¹; using the stoichiometry of the equation instead of the orders.

5. Half-life and first order reactions

The half-life, t½, is the time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half its value.

For a first order reaction the half-life is constant. It doesn't matter what concentration you start from.

This gives you a quick test. Measure two or more half-lives from a concentration–time graph:

  • they're the same → first order
  • they get longer as the reaction goes on → second order
  • they get shorter → zero order

Interactive: measure the half-life from anywhere

orderstart [A]t½ / s

Why is the first order half-life constant?

  1. First order: rate = k[A], so the rate is proportional to the concentration.
  2. Start at 0.80: the rate is high and there's 0.40 to lose. Start at 0.40: the rate is half as fast, but there's only half as much (0.20) to lose.
  3. Half the amount at half the rate takes the same time. So every half-life is equal.
  4. Second order: halve [A] and the rate drops to a quarter, but there is still half as much to lose. So it takes twice as long, and the half-lives get longer.
