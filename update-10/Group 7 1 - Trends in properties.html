Group 7 – Trends in Properties

AQA A-level Chemistry · 3.2.3 Group 7, the halogens · walkthrough 1

1. Meet the halogens

Group 7 elements have 7 outer electrons (s²p⁵). They exist as diatomic molecules: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, held together by a single covalent bond, with van der Waals forces between the molecules.

Click an element.
Colours are a favourite exam question. In water: chlorine pale green, bromine orange/yellow, iodine brown. In an organic solvent like hexane: bromine orange/red, iodine purple.

2. Electronegativity decreases down the group

Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

Down the group: atoms get bigger (more shells), so the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and there is more shielding. The attraction between the nucleus and the bonding pair is weaker, so electronegativity decreases.

The nuclear charge goes up too, but the extra shells and shielding outweigh it.

CHECK 1AQA AS Paper 1, June 2023, Q06.1

Explain why the electronegativity of the halogens decreases down the group. [2 marks]

Atoms get bigger / more shells / more shielding (M1)
so the attraction between the nucleus and the bonding pair of electrons is weaker (M2).
Say "bonding pair" or "shared pair" – not just "electrons".

3. Boiling points increase down the group

Down the group the molecules have more electrons and are bigger, so the van der Waals forces between molecules are stronger. More energy is needed to overcome them, so the boiling point increases.

That's why the states change: F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ is a liquid, I₂ is a solid at room temperature.

Boiling a halogen breaks the van der Waals forces between molecules – not the X–X covalent bond.
CHECK 2AQA AS Paper 1, 2021, Q05.1

Chlorine has a low boiling point because the forces between the molecules are weak. Explain how these forces arise between molecules of chlorine. [3 marks]

Electrons move / are unevenly distributed at any instant, creating a temporary (instantaneous) dipole in one molecule (M1)
this induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule (M2)
the δ+ of one molecule attracts the δ− of the other (M3).
