How buffers work

What a buffer is, the two ways to make an acidic buffer, and how to explain what happens when acid or alkali is added.

What is a buffer?

Buffer solution

A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added

The pH does change, but only very slightly. An acidic buffer (pH below 7) contains a weak acid and its salt. A basic buffer (pH above 7) contains a weak base and its salt.

CHECK 1

Add acid once. By how much does the pH of the water change, compared with the buffer?

The water falls from pH 7.00 to 2.00, a change of 5 units. The buffer falls from 4.76 to 4.67, less than 0.1 of a unit.

CHECK 2

Keep adding acid to the buffer. What happens after about 10 additions, and why?

The pH collapses. All of the A− has been used up, so there is nothing left to remove H+. A buffer only copes with small amounts of acid or alkali.

Two ways to make an acidic buffer

A weak acid on its own has plenty of HA but very little A−. A buffer needs a high concentration of both.

1. Weak acid + its salt

Mix ethanoic acid with sodium ethanoate.

CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO− + H+

The salt is fully dissociated and supplies the A−:

CH3COONa → CH3COO− + Na+

2. Excess weak acid + strong alkali

Add NaOH to an excess of ethanoic acid.

CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O

All of the NaOH reacts. The mixture contains the left-over acid and the salt that has formed.

mol A− formed = mol NaOH added.   mol HA left = mol HA at start − mol NaOH.

CHECK 3

Which mixtures form an acidic buffer?
A: CH3COOH + CH3COONa   B: HCl + NaCl   C: 50 cm3 of 0.1 mol dm−3 CH3COOH + 25 cm3 of 0.1 mol dm−3 NaOH   D: 25 cm3 of 0.1 mol dm−3 CH3COOH + 50 cm3 of 0.1 mol dm−3 NaOH

A and C. A is a weak acid and its salt. In C the weak acid is in excess, so acid and salt are both present. B uses a strong acid. In D the NaOH is in excess, so no weak acid is left.

CHECK 4

Explain why ethanoic acid on its own is not a good buffer.

It is only slightly dissociated, so it contains very few CH3COO− ions. There is not enough A− to remove added H+.

Adding a small amount of acid

HA ⇌ H+ + A−

What happens?

A small amount of HCl is added to the buffer.

1
The added H+ reacts with the A− ions (from the salt):

A− + H+ → HA

2
The equilibrium HA ⇌ H+ + A− shifts to the left, removing most of the added H+.
3
There is a large reservoir of A−, so [A−] falls only slightly and [HA] rises only slightly.
4
The ratio [HA] : [A−] stays almost constant, so [H+] and the pH hardly change.

CHECK 6

Which component of an acidic buffer removes added acid: the weak acid or the salt?

The salt. It supplies the A− ions that react with H+.

Adding a small amount of alkali

HA ⇌ H+ + A−

What happens?

A small amount of NaOH is added to the buffer.

1
The added OH− reacts with the weak acid, HA:

HA + OH− → A− + H2O

2
You can also describe it this way: OH− reacts with H+ to make water, and the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace the H+. Both are accepted.
3
There is a large reservoir of HA, so [HA] falls only slightly and [A−] rises only slightly.
4
The ratio [HA] : [A−] stays almost constant, so the pH hardly changes.

CHECK 7

A buffer contains ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate. Write an equation to show how it removes added OH−.

CH3COOH + OH− → CH3COO− + H2O

CHECK 8

A student writes: "When NaOH is added the equilibrium HA ⇌ H+ + A− shifts to the left." What is wrong?

OH− removes H+ (or HA), so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace the H+.

Why the ratio matters, and other buffers

Ka = [H+][A−][HA]    rearranges to    [H+] = Ka × [HA][A−]

  • Ka is constant at a fixed temperature, so [H+] depends only on the ratio [HA] : [A−].
  • Small additions of acid or alkali barely change the ratio.
  • Adding a little water dilutes HA and A− by the same factor. The ratio, and so the pH, does not change.
  • If [HA] = [A−], then [H+] = Ka and pH = pKa.

Basic buffers

A weak base and its salt, for example NH3 and NH4Cl.

NH3 + H+ → NH4+

NH4+ + OH− → NH3 + H2O

Ammonia removes added acid. Ammonium ions remove added alkali.

Buffers in the blood

Blood is kept between pH 7.35 and 7.45.

H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−

Extra acid (such as lactic acid): HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3, and the equilibrium shifts left.

Extra alkali: H2CO3 + OH− → HCO3− + H2O.

CHECK 9

An ethanoic acid / sodium ethanoate buffer has [HA] = [A−]. Calculate its pH. Ka = 1.74 × 10−5 mol dm−3

[H+] = Ka × 1 = 1.74 × 10−5   pH = pKa = 4.76

CHECK 10

In another buffer [A−] is twice [HA]. Calculate its pH. Ka = 1.74 × 10−5 mol dm−3

[H+] = 1.74 × 10−5 × ½ = 8.70 × 10−6 mol dm−3   pH = 5.06
