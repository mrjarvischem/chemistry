Period 3 oxides: water and acid–base nature

Section 1

First, the elements: sodium and magnesium with water

Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water. Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water, but burns in steam. Notice the products differ: a hydroxide forms with cold water, but an oxide with steam.

Choose a reaction
These are redox reactions. The metal is oxidised (Na: 0 → +1) and hydrogen in water is reduced (+1 → 0):
Na → Na+ + e−
2H2O + 2e− → 2OH− + H2

Aluminium barely reacts with water, because a thin, tough layer of Al2O3 protects it. Rubbing magnesium with sandpaper first speeds up its reaction with cold water for the same reason: it removes the oxide layer.

Section 3

Why are the metal oxides basic?

Na2O and MgO are ionic and contain oxide ions, O2−. An oxide ion is a strong base: it takes a proton from a water molecule, giving hydroxide ions.

O2− + H2O → 2OH−, step by step
Brønsted–Lowry: O2− accepts a proton, so it is the base. H2O donates a proton, so here it is the acid.
Why is MgO solution less alkaline than Na2O solution?

Same amount of oxide added to each beaker. ● metal ions, ● OH− ions.

NaOH is very soluble, so all of it dissolves and [OH−] is high: pH 13–14. Mg(OH)2 is only sparingly soluble. Most stays as a white solid, so [OH−] is much lower: pH about 9–10.

Section 4

Al2O3 and SiO2: no reaction with water

Aluminium oxide

A giant ionic lattice that is too strong to break up in water, so it is insoluble. The pH stays at 7. Even so, it still reacts with acids and bases (Section 7).

Silicon dioxide

A giant covalent lattice. Its strong covalent bonds don't break in water, so it is insoluble and the pH stays at 7. It is still classed as acidic, because it reacts with bases such as hot, concentrated NaOH.

“Insoluble, so no change in pH” doesn't mean “neutral oxide”. Classify an oxide by what it reacts with: Al2O3 is amphoteric and SiO2 is acidic.
Section 5

Why are the non-metal oxides acidic?

P4O10, SO2 and SO3 are covalent molecules, so there is no O2− ion. Instead, the oxygen atoms pull electron density away from the central atom, leaving it δ+. A lone pair on a water molecule attacks it. The product is an oxoacid with O–H bonds, which releases H+ ions into solution.

SO3 + H2O → H2SO4, step by step

This mechanism goes beyond the specification. It's here to show why the oxide is acidic. You need the equations, not the curly arrows.

In the exam a short answer is enough: non-metal oxides react with water to form acids, which release H+ ions (for example H2SO4 → 2H+ + SO42−).

Section 7

Neutralisation, and amphoteric Al2O3

Basic oxides neutralise acids. Acidic oxides neutralise bases. Both give a salt and water. Amphoteric means able to react as an acid and as a base, and Al2O3 is the amphoteric oxide in Period 3.

Pick an oxide and a reagent

Al2O3 acting as a base

Al2O3 + 6HCl → 2AlCl3 + 3H2O

ionic: Al2O3 + 6H+ → 2Al3+ + 3H2O

Al2O3 acting as an acid

Al2O3 + 2NaOH + 3H2O → 2NaAl(OH)4

ionic: Al2O3 + 2OH− + 3H2O → 2Al(OH)4−

Balancing tip. Count the acidic H atoms the acid can lose (HCl 1, H2SO4 2, H3PO4 3) and the charge on the anion of the salt. Phosphate is PO43−, so P4O10 needs 12NaOH to make 4Na3PO4.
