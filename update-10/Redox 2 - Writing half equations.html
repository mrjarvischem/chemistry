Section 1

Redox: two ways to describe it

At GCSE you described redox with electrons: OIL RIG. At A-level you also use oxidation states. The two descriptions always agree.

In terms of electronsIn terms of oxidation state
Oxidationloss of electrons (Oxidation Is Loss)increases ↑
Reductiongain of electrons (Reduction Is Gain)decreases ↓
Zinc in copper(II) sulfate solution
reduction ↓  Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu
oxidation ↑  Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−
Where the electrons go. In a reduction the electrons are gained, so they're on the left. In an oxidation they are lost, so they're on the right.
Section 2

Simple half equations

① Balance the atoms

Cl2 → 2Cl−

② Count the electrons

electrons = change in oxidation state × atoms changing

③ Put them on the right side

Reduction ↓: left. Oxidation ↑: right.

④ Check the charge

Cl2 + 2e−: −2. 2Cl−: −2 ✓

Sodium burning in chlorine: watch the diatomic molecule
Step through a worked example
For a diatomic molecule, balance the atoms first. Each Cl changes by one, but there are two Cl atoms, so Cl2 needs 2 electrons.
Section 3

Ions containing oxygen, in acid

For ions like MnO4− or VO43−, the oxygen has to go somewhere. In acid you may add three things:

H+

Shows the reaction is in acidic conditions.

H2O

Takes up the oxygen and balances the H+.

e−

Balances the charge.

The order. ① Balance the changing atom. ② Add 2H+ for every O to be removed, on the same side as the O. ③ Add H2O to the other side. ④ Add electrons to balance the charge. ⑤ Check: electrons = change in oxidation state × atoms changing.
Step through a worked example
Two atoms changing: in Cr2O72−, balance Cr first (2Cr3+). Each Cr goes from +6 to +3, and there are two of them, so 6 electrons.
Section 4

Oxidations and hydrogen peroxide

The method is the same for an oxidation, but now the oxygen-containing ion is the product. So the H+ and the electrons both end up on the right, and the water on the left.

Step through a worked example
Hydrogen peroxide works both ways. O in H2O2 is −1, so it can go down to −2 (in water) or up to 0 (in oxygen gas). That makes it both an oxidising agent and a reducing agent.

Section 6

Going further: alkaline conditions

OH− in a half equation means alkaline conditions. The most reliable method: write it in acid first, then neutralise the H+.

① Write it in acid

H+, then H2O, then e−, as before.

② Add OH− to both sides

The same number as there are H+. Then H+ + OH− → H2O.

③ Cancel the water

Take away any H2O that is on both sides.
