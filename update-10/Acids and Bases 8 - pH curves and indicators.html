pH curves and indicators

The four types of pH curve, how to sketch them, how to choose an indicator, and what the half-neutralisation point tells you.

Reading a pH curve

A pH curve shows how the pH changes as an acid and a base are mixed in a titration.

Equivalence point

The point at which exactly enough base has been added to react with all of the acid (mol H+ = mol OH−)

End point

The point at which the indicator changes colour

A suitable indicator makes the end point match the equivalence point.

Four things to mark on every sketch

  1. The starting pH (is the solution in the flask strong or weak?)
  2. The equivalence volume (from the moles: here 25 cm3)
  3. The vertical section and the pH range it covers
  4. The final pH (is the solution being added strong or weak?)

Starting and finishing values to learn

StrongWeak
Acid (0.1 mol dm−3)pH 1about pH 3
Base (0.1 mol dm−3)pH 13about pH 11

In excess, a strong acid or base takes the curve back towards these values. A weak one levels off sooner, at about pH 9 to 10 for NH3 or pH 4 to 5 for a weak acid, because a buffer forms.

CHECK 1

Why is the pH curve for HCl and NaOH almost vertical at the equivalence point?

pH is a log scale. Just before the equivalence point almost no H+ is left, so one drop of NaOH removes it and leaves OH− in excess. [H+] falls by a factor of millions in one drop.

CHECK 2

Use the explorer. Describe the curve when NaOH is added to ethanoic acid.

Starts at about pH 3. Rises gently (a buffer forms). Vertical from about pH 7 to 11 at 25 cm3, with the equivalence point at about pH 9. Levels off towards pH 13.

CHECK 3

Switch to "Acid added to base" with HCl and NH3. Describe the curve.

Starts at about pH 11 (weak base). Falls gently, then vertical from about pH 7 down to 3 at 25 cm3. Levels off at about pH 1 to 2 (strong acid in excess).

CHECK 4

Why is the equivalence point above pH 7 for ethanoic acid and NaOH?

The salt formed contains CH3COO− ions, which are a weak base: CH3COO− + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + OH−. The solution contains a small excess of OH−.

Choosing an indicator

An indicator is itself a weak acid whose two forms have different colours: HIn ⇌ H+ + In−. Each one changes colour over its own narrow pH range.

IndicatorColour in acidpH rangeColour in alkali
methyl orangered3.1 – 4.4yellow
phenolphthaleincolourless8.3 – 10.0pink

The rule

The indicator must change colour completely within the vertical section of the pH curve

Then one drop from the burette takes the indicator right through its colour change, so the end point matches the equivalence point. Go back to the explorer on page 2 and tick the indicators to see which bands sit inside the vertical section.

CHECK 5

A student uses methyl orange for a titration of ethanoic acid with NaOH. What effect does this have on the titre?

The titre is too small. Methyl orange changes colour between pH 3.1 and 4.4, long before the equivalence point, and the change is gradual.

CHECK 6

Three indicators: bromocresol green (3.8–5.4), bromothymol blue (6.0–7.6), thymol blue (8.0–9.6). Which is best for propanoic acid with NaOH? Explain.

Thymol blue. It is a weak acid – strong base titration, so the vertical section is about pH 7 to 11. Only thymol blue has a range completely inside it.

CHECK 7

Which of the three is best for hydrochloric acid with ammonia?

Bromocresol green. Strong acid – weak base, so the vertical section is about pH 3 to 7.

The half-neutralisation point

Weak acid + strong base

When half the equivalence volume of NaOH has been added, half of the HA has been turned into A−.

[HA] = [A−]

Ka = [H+][A−][HA] = [H+]

pH = pKa

To find pKa from a curve: find the equivalence volume, halve it, and read off the pH.

CHECK 8

In a titration of a weak acid with NaOH the equivalence point is at 22.40 cm3. The pH after 11.20 cm3 has been added is 4.87. Calculate Ka.

11.20 cm3 is half of 22.40 cm3, so pKa = 4.87
Ka = 10−4.87 = 1.35 × 10−5 mol dm−3

CHECK 9

25.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 NaOH is added to 50.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid (pKa = 4.76). State the pH.

mol HA left = mol A− formed = 2.50 × 10−3: this is half-neutralisation, so pH = pKa = 4.76

Calculating points on a pH curve

Each region of a weak acid – strong base curve uses a different calculation from the earlier walkthroughs. 0.100 mol dm−3 NaOH is added to 25.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid (Ka = 1.74 × 10−5 mol dm−3).

CHECK 10

Calculate the pH before any NaOH is added.

[H+] = √(1.74 × 10−5 × 0.100) = 1.32 × 10−3 mol dm−3
pH = 2.88

CHECK 11

Calculate the pH after 10.0 cm3 of NaOH has been added.

mol A− = mol NaOH = 1.00 × 10−3   mol HA left = 2.50 × 10−3 − 1.00 × 10−3 = 1.50 × 10−3
[H+] = 1.74 × 10−5 × (1.50 ÷ 1.00) = 2.61 × 10−5 mol dm−3
pH = 4.58

CHECK 12

State the pH after 12.5 cm3 of NaOH has been added.

Half-neutralisation: pH = pKa = −log(1.74 × 10−5) = 4.76

CHECK 13

Calculate the pH after 30.0 cm3 of NaOH has been added.

excess OH− = 3.00 × 10−3 − 2.50 × 10−3 = 5.00 × 10−4 mol in 55.0 cm3
[OH−] = 9.09 × 10−3 mol dm−3   [H+] = 1.10 × 10−12 mol dm−3
pH = 11.96
