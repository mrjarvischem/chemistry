Section 1

Burning the elements in oxygen

Na, Mg, Al, Si, P and S all react with oxygen when heated, and each forms an oxide. You need the equation and the observations for each one. Most give a white solid. Sulfur is the odd one out: its oxide is a gas.

Lower the heated element into a gas jar of oxygen

There is a little universal indicator solution in the bottom of the jar. Watch what happens to it with phosphorus and sulfur, whose oxides dissolve to make acids.

Making SO3. Burning sulfur gives SO2, not SO3. SO2 is oxidised further with oxygen over a vanadium(V) oxide, V2O5, catalyst (about 450 °C), as in the Contact process:
2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g)  V2O5 catalyst
Learn the flame colours as a set: Na yellow/orange, Mg brilliant white, Al white, P white (with white smoke), S blue. Silicon must be heated strongly and reacts slowly.
Section 2

Oxidation states across the period

In these oxides oxygen is −2, so the oxidation state of the Period 3 element is easy to work out. In each of the oxides below the element uses all of its outer electrons, so its oxidation state equals its group number, rising from +1 to +6. SO2 is the exception: sulfur uses only four of its six outer electrons.

Section 3

Three types of structure

Moving across the period, the elements change from metals to non-metals, and the bonding in their oxides changes with them.

A metal combined with oxygen gives an ionic oxide. A non-metal combined with oxygen gives a covalent oxide. Silicon forms a giant covalent lattice; phosphorus and sulfur form small molecules.

Section 4

Ionic oxides: Na2O, MgO and Al2O3

These are giant ionic lattices of metal ions and oxide ions, O2−. Every ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions, with strong electrostatic attraction in all directions. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so the melting points are very high.

Heat the lattice
300 K

Why MgO melts higher than Na2O

Mg2+ has a bigger charge than Na+ and is smaller. So it attracts the O2− ions more strongly. Stronger electrostatic forces need more energy to overcome.

Conducting electricity

As solids they don't conduct: the ions are fixed in place. Molten, the ions are free to move and carry charge, so they conduct.

Never write “molecules” or “intermolecular forces” for an ionic oxide. Write: “strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions”.
Section 5

Silicon dioxide: a giant covalent lattice

Si and O are both non-metals, so SiO2 is covalent. But it is not made of molecules. It is a giant covalent (macromolecular) lattice, like diamond with an oxygen atom between each pair of silicon atoms.

● silicon   ● oxygen. This is one small piece of a lattice that carries on in all directions.

Each Si

is covalently bonded to 4 O atoms, arranged tetrahedrally.

Each O

is bonded to 2 Si atoms. So the ratio is Si : O = 1 : 2, giving SiO2.

To melt it

you must break many strong covalent bonds, which takes a lot of energy. Melting point about 1880 K.

Exam answer for SiO2. Giant covalent lattice → many strong covalent bonds between Si and O atoms → a lot of energy needed to break them → high melting point. It doesn't conduct: there are no ions or delocalised electrons.
Section 6

Simple molecular oxides: P4O10, SO2 and SO3

These are separate molecules. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the forces between molecules are weak van der Waals forces. Melting only has to overcome those weak forces, so melting points are low.

How van der Waals forces arise
Why is P4O10 a solid but SO2 a gas? Both are simple molecular. P4O10 molecules are much larger, with more electrons (140 compared with 32) and a bigger surface area. So the van der Waals forces between P4O10 molecules are stronger and need more energy to overcome.
Melting a molecular oxide does not break covalent bonds. Say “weak van der Waals forces between molecules need little energy to overcome”.
