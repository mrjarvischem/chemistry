Teacher notes

Variables

  • Independent: temperature of the water bath.
  • Dependent: time for the pH to fall far enough to turn cresol red from purple to yellow.
  • Control: volumes and concentrations of milk, lipase and sodium carbonate, number of drops of indicator.

Method this models

  • Sodium carbonate makes the milk alkaline, so cresol red starts purple.
  • Lipase breaks down the fat in milk to fatty acids and glycerol. The fatty acids lower the pH and the indicator turns yellow.
  • Warm the milk mixture and the lipase separately in the water bath, mix, start the clock, and stop it when the colour matches the yellow standard.

Questions to draw out

  • How does this model decay? What do decomposers release that does the same job as the lipase?
  • Why does the rate rise between 5 °C and 40 °C? Why does it fall above that?
  • Two students stop the clock at different shades. How could the end point be made less subjective?
  • Why warm the lipase and milk separately before mixing?
  • Link to food storage: why does milk last longer in a fridge?

The data are simulated. The model puts the optimum for lipase close to 40 °C, with the enzyme denatured by 60 °C; real class results vary.
