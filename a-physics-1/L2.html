Lesson 2 · Stable and unstable nuclei

AQA A-level Physics 3.2.1.2 · about 25 minutes

The strong nuclear force

Protons in a nucleus repel each other electrostatically, and gravity between them is far too weak to hold them together. Something else must hold the nucleus together: the strong nuclear force. It acts between all nucleons (proton–proton, proton–neutron, neutron–neutron).

Separation: fm (1 fm = 10−15 m)
The strong force is attractive up to about 3 fm — this holds the nucleons together against the electrostatic repulsion between protons. It is repulsive below about 0.5 fm — this stops the nucleus collapsing. Beyond ~3 fm it is negligible: it is a very short-range force.

Unstable nuclei: alpha decay

Very large nuclei (lots of protons) are unstable: the strong force between nucleons can’t fully balance the electrostatic repulsion. They can decay by emitting an alpha particle — 2 protons and 2 neutrons, a helium nucleus, ⁴₂α.

Equation

In α decay: nucleon number A falls by 4, proton number Z falls by 2. A new element is formed.

β− decay and decay chains

Nuclei with too many neutrons decay by β− emission. A neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron (the β− particle) and an electron antineutrino.

n → p + e− + νe    so A stays the same, Z goes up by 1.

Lesson summary

  • Strong nuclear force: attractive to ~3 fm, repulsive below ~0.5 fm; keeps the nucleus stable.
  • α decay: AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42α — for large nuclei.
  • β− decay: AZX → AZ+1Y + 0−1β + ν̄e — for neutron-rich nuclei.
  • α particles from one source have one energy; β− particles have a range of energies → the antineutrino was hypothesised to conserve energy.
