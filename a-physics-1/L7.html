What happens when light hits a metal?

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface and electrons may be emitted — called photoelectrons. Use the simulator to discover the rules.

Try these, then open the box

  1. Set zinc. Slowly reduce the wavelength from 700 nm. When do electrons start to appear?
  2. Now set a wavelength where nothing is emitted and turn the intensity to maximum. What happens?
  3. With electrons being emitted, change the intensity. Do the electrons go faster?
  4. Reduce the wavelength further. What happens to the fastest electrons?
The four observations
  1. There is a threshold frequency (a maximum wavelength) for each metal. Below it, no electrons are emitted.
  2. Below the threshold, increasing the intensity makes no difference — still no electrons.
  3. Above the threshold, increasing intensity increases the number of electrons per second, but not their maximum kinetic energy.
  4. The maximum kinetic energy depends on the frequency (higher frequency → faster electrons). Emission is instantaneous.

The classic demo (PS 3.2): zinc plate on a gold-leaf electroscope

The photon explanation

Wave theory says brighter light carries more energy, so any colour should eventually release electrons. It doesn’t. Einstein (1905) explained it using photons.

Key definitions

Work function, φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of the metal.

Threshold frequency, f0: the minimum frequency of radiation that will cause photoelectrons to be emitted. A photon at f0 has exactly enough energy: hf0 = φ.

Why wave theory fails

ObservationPhoton explanation
threshold frequencyone photon gives its energy to one electron; if hf < φ, no single photon can free an electron
intensity doesn’t matter below f0brighter = more photons per second, but each still has hf < φ
instant emissionenergy is delivered in one packet, not built up gradually
“More intense light gives electrons more energy” — wrong. Intensity changes the number of photons (and photoelectrons) per second; frequency changes the energy of each.

The photoelectric equation

hf = φ + Ek(max)

Photon energy in = energy to escape + kinetic energy left over. It is a maximum because electrons from deeper in the metal lose some energy (in collisions) before they escape — so photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies up to Ek(max).

Work functions are often given in eV: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J. Convert to joules before using hf (in J).

Stopping potential

In a photocell, a reverse potential difference slows the photoelectrons down. The stopping potential Vs is the minimum p.d. that stops even the fastest ones — the current falls to zero.

Ek(max) = eVs   so   hf = φ + eVs
Reverse p.d.: V

Why does the current fall gradually?

Photoelectrons leave with a range of kinetic energies. As the reverse p.d. increases, electrons lose KE (gain electric potential energy) crossing to the anode, so fewer of them have enough KE to get there. Only at Vs are even the fastest (Ek(max)) stopped.

The simulator uses a simplified spread of energies, so the graph is a straight line. Real I–V curves curve, but they still reach zero at Vs.

Exam practice

h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s, c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1, e = 1.60 × 10−19 C.

Lesson summary

  • One photon → one electron. Photon energy E = hf.
  • Work function φ = minimum energy to remove an electron from the surface. Threshold frequency f0 = φ/h.
  • hf = φ + Ek(max). Intensity changes the number of electrons per second, not Ek(max).
  • Ek(max) = eVs. Range of KE → current falls gradually as reverse p.d. rises.
