Metals have giant structures with strong metallic bonding, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome the bonds. This means most metals have high melting and boiling points.

Reading the diagram

  • Dashed circle with +: the nucleus and inner shells. Once the outer electrons have gone, this is the positive ion.
  • Solid outer circle: the outer shell of each atom, with its electron(s) ⊖.
  • Grey shading: the delocalised electrons, free to move through the whole structure.

Strengths and limitations

  • ✓ Shows the regular arrangement of positive ions.
  • ✓ Shows the delocalised electrons are spread through the whole structure, not belonging to one ion.
  • ✗ 2D only, and shows only a few ions of a giant structure.
  • ✗ The shading does not show how many electrons there are, or that they move.
  • ✗ The attraction between the ions and the electrons is not shown.

Why metals conduct

  • Electricity: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the whole structure, so they carry electrical charge through the metal.
  • Thermal energy: energy is transferred through the metal by the delocalised electrons.
  • The positive ions stay in their fixed positions; they only vibrate.

Examples of alloys

  • Brass: copper with zinc
  • Bronze: copper with tin
  • Steel: iron with carbon and other elements
  • Aluminium alloys: aluminium with copper or magnesium

Pure metals are too soft for many uses, so they are mixed with other metals to make alloys, which are harder.
