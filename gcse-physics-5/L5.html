Lesson 5 · Forces and elasticity

AQA GCSE Physics / Combined Science · Forces 4.5.3 · Required practical · about 25 minutes

Stretching, squashing and bending

To change the shape of a stationary object you need more than one force — e.g. a spring is stretched by your pull and held by the clamp.

Elastic deformation

The object goes back to its original shape and length when the forces are removed. e.g. a spring with a small load.

Inelastic deformation

The object does not go back to its original shape. e.g. modelling clay, or a spring stretched too far.

Force and extension

F = k e Learn this — not on the equation sheet
SymbolQuantityUnit
Fforcenewtons, N
kspring constantnewtons per metre, N/m
eextensionmetres, m

The extension of an elastic object is directly proportional to the force — as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded. The same applies to compression.

A force that stretches (or compresses) a spring does work, and elastic potential energy is stored. If the spring isn’t inelastically deformed, the work done = elastic potential energy stored: Ee = ½ k e² (on the equation sheet).

Required practical: force and extension of a spring

Hang 1 N weights on a spring one at a time. Read the ruler at the pointer each time and work out the extension.

Read the ruler at eye level using a fixed pointer to avoid parallax. Extension = new length − original length — not just the ruler reading.

Exam practice

Lesson summary

  • Changing the shape of a stationary object needs more than one force.
  • Elastic: returns to its original shape. Inelastic: doesn’t.
  • F = k e (learn it). Extension ∝ force up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Gradient of the force–extension graph (straight part) = k. Work done stretching = ½ k e².
