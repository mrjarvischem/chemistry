Natural sources: rocks (including radon gas) and cosmic rays from space.
Man-made sources: medical uses, fallout from nuclear weapons testing and nuclear accidents.

Radiation dose

The radiation dose measures the risk of harm from radiation absorbed by the body. It is measured in sieverts (Sv). Doses are usually tiny, so we use millisieverts: 1000 mSv = 1 Sv.

The background dose depends on where you live (e.g. areas with granite rock give off more radon) and your job (e.g. airline crew get more cosmic radiation; radiographers and nuclear workers are monitored).

Why half-life matters

Short half-life

Decays quickly: a high activity at first (more dangerous while it lasts), but it soon becomes safe.

Long half-life

Lower activity, but it stays radioactive for a very long time — e.g. nuclear waste must be stored safely for thousands of years.

Using radiation in medicine

Nuclear radiation is used to explore internal organs (tracers) and to control or destroy unwanted tissue such as tumours (radiotherapy). Pick the best isotope for a tracer injected into a patient and detected from outside.

Choose an isotope.
A good tracer: emits gamma (passes out of the body to the detector, weakly ionising) and has a short half-life (hours) — long enough to do the scan, short enough that the patient isn’t exposed for long. Doctors weigh up the benefit against the risk.

Exam practice

Lesson summary

  • Background radiation: natural (rocks, radon, cosmic rays) and man-made (medical, fallout, accidents).
  • Dose in sieverts (1000 mSv = 1 Sv); depends on location and occupation.
  • Short half-life: intense but brief. Long half-life: hazardous for a long time.
  • Tracers: gamma emitter, short half-life. Radiotherapy: radiation destroys tumour cells. Weigh benefits against risks.
