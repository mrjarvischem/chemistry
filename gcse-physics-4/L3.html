Lesson 3 · Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation

AQA GCSE Physics / Combined Science · Atomic structure 4.4.2.1–4.4.2.2 · about 25 minutes

Radioactive decay

Some atomic nuclei are unstable. They give out radiation as they change to become more stable. This is radioactive decay, and it is a random process — you can’t predict when a particular nucleus will decay.

Activity

The activity of a source is the rate at which it decays: the number of decays per second. Unit: the becquerel (Bq). 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.

Count rate

A Geiger–Müller tube and counter detect radiation. The count rate is the number of decays recorded each second (or minute) by the detector — usually less than the activity, because not all the radiation reaches the tube.

Types of nuclear radiation

Alpha particle (α)

2 protons + 2 neutrons — the same as a helium nucleus. Charge +2. Strongly ionising.

Beta particle (β)

A high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton. Charge −1. Moderately ionising.

Gamma ray (γ)

Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus. No charge, no mass. Weakly ionising.

Neutron (n)

Some nuclei emit a neutron. No charge.

Ionising means knocking electrons off atoms, turning them into ions. This is what makes radiation harmful to living cells. Alpha is the most ionising; gamma the least.

Exam practice

Lesson summary

  • Unstable nuclei decay randomly, giving out radiation. Activity = decays per second, in becquerels (Bq).
  • Count rate = counts per second measured by a Geiger–Müller tube.
  • α: 2p + 2n, stopped by paper, few cm of air, strongly ionising. β: fast electron from the nucleus, stopped by a few mm of aluminium. γ: EM wave, reduced by thick lead, weakly ionising.
  • Some nuclei emit neutrons.
