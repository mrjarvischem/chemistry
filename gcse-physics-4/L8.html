Nuclear fission

Fission is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus such as uranium or plutonium. Step through it.

Spontaneous fission is rare. Usually the nucleus must first absorb a neutron. It then splits into two smaller nuclei (roughly equal in size), and emits two or three neutrons plus gamma rays. Energy is released: all the fission products have kinetic energy.

Chain reactions

The neutrons released can cause more fissions. Start a chain reaction, then try it with control rods.

Fissions in each generation

In a nuclear reactor the chain reaction is controlled: control rods absorb some neutrons so that, on average, each fission causes just one more. In a nuclear weapon the chain reaction is uncontrolled, releasing a huge amount of energy almost instantly.

Nuclear fusion

Fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus. In this process some of the mass may be converted into the energy of radiation.

Fusion powers the Sun and other stars. On Earth it is very hard to achieve: the nuclei are both positive and repel, so they need extremely high temperatures and pressures to get close enough to fuse.

Fission

Splits a large nucleus. Used in today’s power stations.

Fusion

Joins small nuclei. Powers stars; research reactors on Earth.

Exam practice

Lesson summary

  • Fission: a large unstable nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, 2–3 neutrons and gamma rays. Energy released.
  • The neutrons can cause further fissions: a chain reaction.
  • Reactor: controlled (control rods absorb neutrons). Weapon: uncontrolled.
  • Fusion: two light nuclei join; some mass becomes energy of radiation. Powers the Sun.
