Walkthrough 5 · Neutralisation of acids and salt production

AQA GCSE Chemistry 4.4.2.2 · Combined Science 5.4.2.2 · about 30 minutes

What neutralises an acid?

Acids are neutralised by bases and by carbonates.

Bases

Metal oxides and metal hydroxides. They react with acids to make a salt and water. Examples: copper oxide {CuO}, magnesium hydroxide {Mg(OH)2}.

Alkalis

Bases that dissolve in water — soluble metal hydroxides, e.g. sodium hydroxide {NaOH}, potassium hydroxide {KOH}. They make {OH^-} ions in solution.

Carbonates

Metal carbonates, e.g. calcium carbonate {CaCO3}. They also make carbon dioxide, so they fizz.

All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. Copper oxide is a base but it is insoluble, so it is not an alkali.

Three general equations

acid + metal oxide→ salt + water
acid + metal hydroxide→ salt + water
acid + metal carbonate→ salt + water + carbon dioxide

Build a reaction

Test for carbon dioxide: bubble it through limewater — the limewater turns milky (cloudy).

Working out the formula of a salt

A salt is an ionic compound: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Pick two ions and see how many of each are needed — the bars must be the same length.

Use brackets when you need more than one of a polyatomic ion: {Ca(NO3)2}, not {CaNO32}. Make sure you know: {OH^-}, {NO3^-}, {SO4^2-}, {CO3^2-}.

Random neutralisation reactions. Type the coefficient (big number) for each formula; a blank box counts as 1.

Quiz and exam practice

Summary

  • Bases = metal oxides and hydroxides. Alkalis = soluble bases (soluble hydroxides).
  • acid + metal oxide/hydroxide → salt + water.
  • acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide (limewater turns milky).
  • Salt formula: balance the charges of the ions; brackets for more than one polyatomic ion.
