Acids and pH Explained

Page 1

What makes something an acid?

When an acid dissolves in water, its molecules can split up into ions. Every acid makes the same ion: the hydrogen ion, H⁺.

It is the H⁺ ions that make a solution acidic. More H⁺ ions in the same volume of solution means a more acidic solution.

Key word

Ionise: when a molecule splits into ions. For an acid, HA → H⁺ + A⁻.

Think: hydrochloric acid is HCl. Which two ions does it make when it ionises?

A strong acid is completely ionised in water. Every molecule splits into ions. Examples: hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acid.

We've exaggerated the numbers so you can see them. In real vinegar-strength acid, only about 1 molecule in 75 splits up.

Key idea

A weak acid is only partially ionised in water. The reaction is reversible (⇌): ions keep joining back together while other molecules split, so the number of H⁺ ions stays about the same. Examples: ethanoic, citric and carbonic acid.

Watch out: strong does NOT mean concentrated

Strong or weak is about what fraction of the molecules ionise.

Concentrated or dilute is about how much acid is dissolved in each dm³ of water.

So you can have a dilute strong acid, or a concentrated weak acid.

pH tells you how many H⁺ ions there are

Here is the tricky part. The more H⁺ ions there are, the lower the pH. When one goes up, the other goes down. This is called an inverse relationship.

More H⁺ ions ⇒ lower pHmore acidic
Fewer H⁺ ions ⇒ higher pHless acidic, closer to 7

Key idea

Each time the pH goes down by 1, there are 10 times more H⁺ ions. Each time it goes up by 1, there are 10 times fewer.

Bigger jumps: multiply by 10 each step

If the pH changes by more than 1, multiply by 10 for each step.

Tip: count the number of 10s, or count the zeros. A pH change of 3 gives 1 followed by 3 zeros: 1000.

Worked example: lemon juice has pH 2 and coffee has pH 5. Which has more H⁺ ions, and how many times more?
Lemon juice has the lower pH, so it has more H⁺. The difference is 5 − 2 = 3 steps: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 times more.
Page 7

Adding water: dilution

Diluting × 10 means making the volume 10 times bigger with water, for example 10 cm³ of acid made up to 100 cm³. The same H⁺ ions are spread through 10 times the space, so there are 10 times fewer in each cm³. The pH goes up by 1.

Exam trick

pH values like 1.3 follow the same rule. 0.050 mol/dm³ has pH 1.3, so diluting × 10 gives pH 2.3, and × 100 gives pH 3.3. Just add 1 for each × 10. No calculator needed.

Page 9

Why stronger acids react faster

Magnesium reacts with the H⁺ ions, not with the whole acid molecules:

Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)

Add the acids, then the magnesium. Watch which beaker fizzes fastest.

Key idea

At the same concentration, a stronger acid has more H⁺ ions in the same volume. More H⁺ ions means more frequent collisions with the magnesium, so the rate of reaction is faster.

Keep watching: all four beakers end up making the same amount of hydrogen. In a weak acid, when H⁺ ions get used up, more molecules ionise to replace them. It just takes longer.
Page 10

Summary

  • Acids produce H⁺ ions in water.
  • A strong acid is completely ionised. A weak acid is only partially ionised (reversible, ⇌).
  • Strong/weak = fraction ionised. Concentrated/dilute = amount of acid per dm³.
  • More H⁺ = lower pH. pH down by 1 = 10 times more H⁺.
  • A pH change of 2 = 100 times; 3 = 1000 times.
  • Diluting × 10 raises the pH by 1, but the pH can never go above 7 by adding water.
  • At the same concentration, stronger acids have more H⁺, so they react faster (more frequent collisions).
